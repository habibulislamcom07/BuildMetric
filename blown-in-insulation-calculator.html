Quick Answer: How Do I Calculate Blown-In Insulation?

To calculate blown-in insulation, first find your attic area (Length × Width). Determine your desired R-Value (usually R-38 to R-60 in the US). Check the specific bag's coverage chart (a standard 25lb bag of fiberglass covers about 40-50 sq ft at R-38). Divide your total area by the bag's coverage. Finally, factor in any existing insulation already in the attic. Our calculator above handles this complex math automatically.

The Engineering of Thermal Insulation

Insulation is not just about keeping your house warm; it is a critical component of the building envelope. In the winter, it prevents heat from escaping. In the summer, it prevents the blistering heat of the attic from radiating into your living room.

Blown-in insulation (often fiberglass or cellulose) is the preferred method for retrofitting existing attics because it can be blown into tight corners, around obstructions, and over existing joists without tearing out drywall.

However, insulation only works if it is applied at the correct depth. If you compress the insulation, you destroy the tiny air pockets that provide thermal resistance. This is why you cannot simply "fluff" the insulation; you must install it to the precise depth required to achieve your target R-Value. If your attic needs new framing or support for heavier insulation, check out our framing calculator.

R-Value vs. U-Value: US vs. UK vs. Australia

This is the most confusing part of insulation for international builders. The United States and Australia use the R-Value (Thermal Resistance). The higher the number, the better the insulation.

The United Kingdom uses the U-Value (Thermal Transmittance). The lower the number, the better the insulation.

If you are using American tutorials while building in the UK, you must convert your R-Values to U-Values. Our calculator allows you to target the specific R-Value required by your local jurisdiction (IRC in the US, Approved Document L in the UK, and NCC in Australia).

Region Standard Unit Typical Attic Target Governing Code
USA R-Value (Higher is Better) R-38 to R-60 (Approx 12" - 18" depth) IRC (International Residential Code)
UK U-Value (Lower is Better) 0.11 to 0.16 W/m²K (Approx 270mm - 400mm depth) Approved Document L (Building Regs)
Australia R-Value (Higher is Better) R-3.5 to R-6.0 (Approx 120mm - 200mm) NCC (National Construction Code)

The Math: How to Calculate Bag Yield

One of the biggest mistakes DIYers make is buying insulation based on square footage alone. You must calculate based on the desired depth.

Fiberglass and cellulose settle over time. A bag that claims to cover 50 sq ft at R-38 might only cover 40 sq ft at R-49.

Example Calculation:
Attic Area: 1,000 sq ft.
Desired R-Value: R-49.
Manufacturer Bag Yield: 35 sq ft per bag at R-49.
Total Bags = 1,000 ÷ 35 = 28.5 bags (round up to 29 bags).

Our calculator uses the most common industry standards (such as a standard 25lb fiberglass bag yielding roughly 40-50 sq ft at R-38) but allows you to select your bag weight. Always double-check the manufacturer's specific chart on the bag before purchasing, as fiberglass density varies by brand. For heavy loads in the attic, you might also need to verify the ceiling joist strength with our joist calculator.

Existing Insulation: The Hidden Factor

If your attic already has 4 inches of old fiberglass, you don't need to buy as much new insulation to reach R-49.

However, you must be careful. If the existing insulation is damaged, wet, or compressed (like old batt insulation), it provides very little R-value. Furthermore, you cannot put new blown-in insulation over a radiant barrier (foil) or over wet insulation.

Assuming the old insulation is in good condition, our calculator automatically subtracts the existing R-value from your target. For example, if you have existing R-11 (approx 3.5 inches) and want R-49, the calculator will only estimate the bags needed to add the remaining R-38. If you are installing a new ceiling beneath the insulation, use our drywall calculator.

Installation Best Practices: Baffles and Ventilation

Blowing insulation into an attic is not as simple as just pointing a hose and pulling the trigger. Proper installation requires careful preparation to ensure the insulation does its job without causing moisture problems.

1. Install Baffles at the Eaves

The most common mistake DIYers make is blowing insulation directly against the roof deck at the eaves, blocking the soffit vents. If you block the intake vents, your attic cannot breathe. This traps moisture, leading to mold, rot, and ice dams in cold climates.

You must install Baffles (also called rafter vents or insulation stops) at every rafter bay where there is a soffit vent. These foam or plastic channels maintain a 1-inch to 2-inch gap between the insulation and the roof deck, allowing air to flow freely from the soffit to the ridge vent.

2. Air Sealing (The Invisible Barrier)

Insulation slows down heat transfer, but it does not stop air movement. If you have gaps around recessed lights, plumbing penetrations, or the top plates of your walls, warm air will bypass the insulation entirely (the "wind wash" effect).

Before blowing any insulation, use expanding spray foam or caulk to seal every gap. Use IC-rated (Insulation Contact) recessed light covers to prevent fire hazards. For sealing larger gaps in the walls below, see our drywall calculator for patching materials.

3. Vapor Barriers

In cold climates (US), a vapor barrier (usually polyethylene plastic) is installed on the warm side of the insulation to prevent warm, moist interior air from entering the attic and condensing on the cold roof deck. However, if you are blowing loose-fill insulation over an existing vapor barrier, ensure it is intact. In the UK and Australia, breathable membranes are often used instead of strict vapor barriers.

Safety and Equipment: Protecting Yourself

Blowing insulation is a messy, dusty job. Fiberglass and cellulose fibers are irritants to the skin, eyes, and lungs.

  • Respirator: Wear a high-quality N95 or P100 respirator. Never wear a simple dust mask.
  • PPE: Wear long sleeves, long pants, gloves, and tight-fitting goggles (not just glasses) to prevent fiber itch and eye irritation.
  • Equipment: You can rent insulation blowers from major hardware stores (Home Depot, Lowe's in the US; Tool Hire shops in the UK/AU). These machines are powerful and require two people: one to feed the machine and one in the attic directing the hose.
  • Attic Safety: Always walk on the ceiling joists or lay down plywood walking boards. Stepping between the joists can result in falling through the ceiling. Ensure the attic is well-lit and well-ventilated during the process.

Costing and Energy Savings

Adding blown-in insulation is one of the highest ROI (Return on Investment) home improvement projects you can do.

In the US, a standard 25lb bag of fiberglass costs around $30 to $50. For a 1,000 sq ft attic, a DIY R-49 project might cost $500 to $800 in materials (plus machine rental). A professional contractor might charge $1,500 to $3,000.

Depending on your climate zone, upgrading your attic insulation can reduce your heating and cooling bills by 15% to 30% per year. Many regions (US, UK, AU) also offer government rebates or tax credits for energy-efficient upgrades. Always check local utility company websites for incentives before buying materials. Frequently Asked Questions

Blown-In Insulation FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about R-values, depth, and installation.

01

How many bags of blown-in insulation for 1,000 sq ft?
For a 1,000 sq ft attic targeting R-49 (a common standard), you will typically need 20 to 25 bags of standard 25lb fiberglass insulation. However, this depends entirely on the brand's specific bag yield and your existing insulation depth. Use the calculator above with your exact target R-value for a precise count.
02

How deep should blown-in insulation be?
Depth dictates your R-Value. In the US, R-38 usually requires about 12-14 inches, while R-49 requires 14-16 inches, and R-60 requires 16-18 inches. In the UK, targeting a U-Value of 0.11 might require 270mm to 400mm (10-15 inches) of mineral wool. The insulation must not be compressed to achieve these depths.
03

Can I put blown-in insulation over existing fiberglass?
Yes, you can add blown-in insulation over existing batt insulation, as long as the existing insulation is dry, mold-free, and not severely compressed. It is a great way to top up R-value. However, never install a vapor barrier over existing insulation; doing so can trap moisture and cause rot. For ceiling framing checks, use our joist calculator.
04

What is the difference between R-Value and U-Value?
R-Value measures thermal resistance (how well the material resists heat flow). Higher is better. It is used in the US and Australia. U-Value measures thermal transmittance (how much heat passes through). Lower is better. It is used in the UK. To convert, U-Value = 1 / R-Value (using metric units).
05

Do I need baffles for blown-in insulation?
Yes, absolutely. If your attic has soffit vents at the eaves, you must install rafter baffles. Baffles keep the insulation from blocking the airflow from the soffit to the ridge. Blocking this airflow traps moisture, causes mold, and creates ice dams in winter.
06

How much does blown-in insulation cost?
For DIY installation, a 25lb bag of fiberglass costs roughly $30-$50 in the US. Renting the blowing machine is often free if you buy a minimum number of bags. Professional installation can cost $1,500 to $3,000 for an average attic, but you may qualify for energy rebates. For more home improvement budgets, check our framing calculator.
07

Is fiberglass or cellulose blown-in insulation better?
Both are excellent. Fiberglass is fire-resistant and doesn't settle much over time. Cellulose (made from recycled paper) is treated with fire retardants, is denser, and does a slightly better job at blocking airflow. However, cellulose can be messier and requires more bags due to its higher density. If you spot an error in our guide, please report it to our team.
