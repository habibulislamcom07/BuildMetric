What Is a Paint Calculator?

A paint calculator is a tool that estimates how much paint you need to cover a given area — measured in litres, gallons or the number of paint cans required. Instead of guessing and ending up with too little paint (which means a trip back to the store and a visible mismatch in colour or finish) or too much (which means wasted money sitting in your garage), you enter your room dimensions and the calculator works out the exact quantity.

Paint quantity depends on several factors: the total surface area to be painted (walls, ceiling and trim), the number of coats you plan to apply, the coverage rate of the paint you are using, the surfaces that do not need painting (such as doors and windows), and whether you plan to apply a primer coat first. This calculator takes all of these into account so you get a reliable estimate before you buy.

Paint is one of the most impactful and cost-effective ways to transform a space. It protects surfaces, improves appearance and can dramatically change the mood of a room. Whether you are repainting a bedroom, refreshing a living room, painting an entire house exterior, or touching up a rental property between tenants, accurate paint estimation saves time, money and frustration.

Whether you are painting an apartment in New York, a Victorian terrace in London, a modern home in Sydney, or a rental unit in Toronto, this calculator gives you an accurate estimate in both imperial and metric units.

How to Use This Paint Calculator

Using this paint calculator is straightforward. Follow these seven steps:

  1. Enter the room dimensions. Provide the length, width and wall height of the room in your chosen unit system. For irregularly shaped rooms, divide the space into rectangles, calculate each area separately, and add the results together.
  2. Select doors and windows. Use the dropdowns to indicate how many doors and windows are on the walls you are painting. The calculator subtracts their typical area automatically — a standard door is about 20 sq ft (1.8 m²) and a standard window is about 16 sq ft (1.5 m²).
  3. Choose the number of coats. Two coats are standard for most interior walls. Dark colours or drastic colour changes may need three coats.
  4. Select the paint type. Interior wall paint and exterior wall paint have slightly different coverage rates, so choose the correct one for your project.
  5. Include the ceiling (optional). If you are painting the ceiling as well as the walls, choose "Yes — include ceiling." The calculator adds the ceiling area to your total.
  6. Include primer (optional). If you need a primer coat (for bare drywall, drastic colour changes or stained surfaces), choose "Yes — include primer coat." The calculator adds primer to your total.
  7. Click Calculate. The result shows the total paintable area, the paint needed per coat, the total paint required, and the number of standard paint cans you should buy.

Pro Tip: Always buy slightly more paint than the calculator suggests. Paint colours can vary slightly between batches, so having a small reserve for touch-ups is essential. If you have leftover paint, store it in a sealed container, label it with the room and colour, and keep it for future touch-ups.

Formula Used by This Calculator

All calculations are performed internally in metric units, then converted back to your preferred unit system for display. The following formulas are used:

Step 1 — Wall Area

Wall Area = 2 × (Length + Width) × Height This gives the total wall surface area of a rectangular room, in square units.

Step 2 — Ceiling Area (if included)

Ceiling Area = Length × Width Only add this if you are painting the ceiling as well as the walls.

Step 3 — Subtract Doors and Windows

Net Area = (Wall Area + Ceiling Area) − (Doors × 20) − (Windows × 16) Standard door: 20 sq ft (1.8 m²). Standard window: 16 sq ft (1.5 m²).

Step 4 — Paint Quantity

Paint per Coat = Net Area ÷ Coverage Rate Interior coverage: ~10 m² per litre (~350 sq ft per gallon)
Exterior coverage: ~8 m² per litre (~300 sq ft per gallon)

Step 5 — Total Paint Needed

Total Paint = Paint per Coat × Number of Coats Standard recommendation: 2 coats.

Step 6 — Primer (if included)

Primer = Paint per Coat × 1 One coat of primer covers the same area as one coat of paint.

Step 7 — Recommended Cans

Cans = Total Paint ÷ Can Size Common can sizes: 1 L, 4 L, 5 L (metric) or 1 gallon (imperial).
Always round up to the nearest full can.

Understanding the Variables

  • Wall Area: Total wall surface area, excluding ceiling.
  • Ceiling Area: Ceiling surface area (only if painting the ceiling).
  • Door/Window Area: Standard deductions for openings.
  • Coverage Rate: The area one litre or gallon of paint covers in a single coat.
  • Number of Coats: How many times you plan to apply paint.
  • Primer: An optional base coat applied before paint.

Why Round Up, Not Down?

Paint calculations involve rounding in two places: total paint and can count. Always round up, never down. If your calculation gives 5.2 litres, buy 6 litres. If it gives 1.4 gallons, buy 1.5 gallons. Running short mid-project is far more expensive than having spare paint at the end — especially if the store's next batch has a slightly different colour.

Coverage Rates Vary by Paint Type

Not all paints cover the same area. Premium paints typically cover more area per litre than budget paints. Similarly, darker colours and high-gloss finishes may require additional coats, reducing effective coverage. Always check the manufacturer's stated coverage on the paint can. Most interior wall paints cover 10–12 m² per litre (350–400 sq ft per gallon). Exterior paints typically cover 8–10 m² per litre (300–350 sq ft per gallon) because exterior surfaces are more porous. Primers cover 10–12 m² per litre. If you are painting over a dark colour with a light one, expect to need an extra coat, which reduces the effective coverage rate.

Worked Example

Let us work through three examples — a small bedroom, a larger living room with ceiling, and an exterior wall.

Example 1 — Small Bedroom (10 ft × 12 ft × 8 ft, 1 door, 2 windows, 2 coats, interior)

  1. Convert to metres: 10 ft = 3.05 m, 12 ft = 3.66 m, 8 ft = 2.44 m
  2. Wall area: 2 × (3.05 + 3.66) × 2.44 = 32.74 m²
  3. Subtract door: 32.74 − 1.8 = 30.94 m²
  4. Subtract windows: 30.94 − (2 × 1.5) = 27.94 m²
  5. Paint per coat (interior): 27.94 ÷ 10 = 2.79 litres
  6. Total paint (2 coats): 2.79 × 2 = 5.59 litres
  7. Bags needed (5L cans): 1 × 5L can + 1 × 1L can

Result: You need approximately 5.6 litres of paint — or about 1.5 gallons in imperial units. Buy one 5-litre can and one 1-litre can to be safe.

Example 2 — Living Room with Ceiling (4 m × 5 m × 2.7 m, 1 door, 3 windows, 2 coats, interior + ceiling)

  1. Wall area: 2 × (4 + 5) × 2.7 = 48.6 m²
  2. Ceiling area: 4 × 5 = 20 m²
  3. Subtract door: 48.6 + 20 − 1.8 = 66.8 m²
  4. Subtract windows: 66.8 − (3 × 1.5) = 62.3 m²
  5. Paint per coat (interior): 62.3 ÷ 10 = 6.23 litres
  6. Total paint (2 coats): 6.23 × 2 = 12.46 litres
  7. Recommended cans: 2 × 5L cans + 1 × 4L can

Result: You need approximately 12.5 litres of paint — or about 3.3 gallons. Buy two 5-litre cans and one 4-litre can (or three 5-litre cans for extra reserve).

Example 3 — Exterior Wall (30 ft × 20 ft, 2 doors, 4 windows, 2 coats, exterior, 20% waste for texture)

  1. Convert to metres: 30 ft = 9.14 m, 20 ft = 6.10 m
  2. Wall area: 2 × (9.14 + 6.10) × 3.05 (10 ft height) = 92.98 m²
  3. Subtract door: 92.98 − (2 × 1.8) = 89.38 m²
  4. Subtract windows: 89.38 − (4 × 1.5) = 83.38 m²
  5. Paint per coat (exterior): 83.38 ÷ 8 = 10.42 litres
  6. Total paint (2 coats): 10.42 × 2 = 20.84 litres
  7. Add 10% texture waste: 20.84 × 1.10 = 22.93 litres

Result: You need approximately 23 litres of exterior paint — or about 6 gallons. Buy five 5-litre cans or six 1-gallon cans.

Pro Tip: For textured surfaces (stucco, brick, rough wood siding), add 15–25% extra paint. Texture absorbs more paint in the first coat, and coverage is lower than on smooth surfaces. For the second coat, coverage improves significantly. For heavily textured surfaces, use a sprayer instead of a roller for better penetration.

Types of Paint

Not all paint is the same. Different types are designed for different surfaces and conditions:

  • Water-Based (Latex) Paint: Most common interior paint. Easy to apply, quick drying, low odour. Clean-up with water. Suitable for walls, ceilings and most interior surfaces.
  • Oil-Based (Alkyd) Paint: Durable, hard-wearing finish. Excellent for trim, doors and cabinets. Longer drying time and strong odour. Clean-up with mineral spirits.
  • Primer: Applied before paint to seal surfaces and improve adhesion. Essential for bare drywall, patched surfaces and dramatic colour changes.
  • Enamel Paint: Hard, glossy finish. Used for trim, doors and high-traffic areas. Available in water-based and oil-based formulas.
  • Matte / Flat Paint: Non-reflective finish. Hides surface imperfections. Ideal for ceilings, low-traffic rooms and adult bedrooms.
  • Eggshell Paint: Slight sheen. Easy to clean. Best for living rooms, hallways and family rooms.
  • Satin Paint: Soft sheen. Durable and washable. Used for kitchens, bathrooms and trim.
  • Semi-Gloss Paint: Shiny finish. Highly durable and easy to clean. Used for trim, doors, cabinets and bathrooms.
  • Gloss Paint: Highest shine. Most durable. Used for trim, doors and furniture. Highlights surface imperfections.
  • Exterior Paint: Formulated for weather resistance. Resists UV, moisture and temperature changes. Used for siding, fences and outdoor structures.
  • Specialty Paint: Includes chalkboard, magnetic, metallic, masonry and fire-retardant paints for specific applications.

Industry Standards and Codes

Paint products must comply with several industry standards and environmental regulations:

  • ASTM D16: Standard Terminology for Paint, Related Coatings, Materials and Applications
  • ASTM D523: Standard Test Method for Specular Gloss
  • ASTM D2486: Standard Test Methods for Scrub Resistance of Wall Paints
  • ASTM D3359: Standard Test Methods for Measuring Adhesion by Tape Test
  • ASTM D3960: Standard Practice for Determining Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) Content of Paints
  • ISO 11890: Paints and Varnishes — Determination of VOC Content
  • Green Seal GS-11: Standard for Paints, Coatings, Stains and Sealers (environmental certification)
  • GREENGUARD Gold: Certification for low chemical emissions

Always check local regulations for VOC limits before purchasing paint. Many regions (including the EU, California and parts of Asia) have strict limits on VOC content for both interior and exterior paints. Low-VOC and zero-VOC paints are widely available and recommended for interior use, especially in bedrooms and children's rooms.

Paint quality matters more than brand — a mid-range paint often performs as well as a premium paint for interior walls.

Tools and Equipment Needed

To paint a room efficiently, you will need the following tools:

  • Essential: Paint rollers (9-inch for walls, 4-inch for trim), roller covers (3/8-inch nap for smooth walls, 1/2-inch for textured), angled paint brush (2–2.5 inch), paint tray, paint can opener, stirring stick
  • Preparation: Painter's tape, drop cloths, sandpaper (120–220 grit), spackle, putty knife, caulk gun, cleaning rags
  • Safety: Dust mask, safety glasses, gloves, ventilation (open windows and doors)
  • Optional: Extension pole (for ceilings and high walls), paint sprayer, paint edger, ladder or step stool

Safety first: paint fumes can be harmful. Always paint in a well-ventilated area, take breaks, and avoid painting in enclosed spaces without proper ventilation. Wear a dust mask when sanding and a respirator when using oil-based paints. Keep children and pets away from freshly painted areas.

Maintenance and Care

Proper maintenance extends the life of your paint job:

  • Allow proper curing: Paint takes 7–30 days to fully cure. Avoid washing walls for at least 2 weeks after painting.
  • Clean gently: Use a soft sponge and mild soap. Avoid abrasive cleaners and rough scrubbing, especially on flat finishes.
  • Touch up promptly: Keep leftover paint for touch-ups. Store in a sealed container, label with room, colour and date.
  • Check for moisture: Peeling or bubbling paint indicates moisture problems. Fix leaks before repainting.
  • Repaint every 5–10 years: Interior walls need repainting every 5–10 years. High-traffic areas may need more frequent repainting.
  • Use low-VOC paint: Low-VOC and zero-VOC paints have less odour and are safer for indoor air quality.
  • Proper storage: Store paint in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. Keep cans sealed tightly.
  • Dispose properly: Never pour paint down drains. Take leftover paint to a local hazardous waste facility.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced painters make mistakes when calculating paint quantities. Here are the most common ones:

  • Forgetting to subtract doors and windows: These openings do not need paint. Subtract them for a more accurate estimate.
  • Applying only one coat: One coat rarely provides full coverage. Two coats are standard.
  • Using the wrong coverage rate: Interior paint covers 10–12 m²/L, exterior 8–10 m²/L. Textured surfaces need 15–25% more.
  • Forgetting primer: Primer is essential for bare drywall, patched surfaces and dramatic colour changes.
  • Buying budget paint: Cheap paint covers less area, requires more coats, and wears faster. Mid-range paint is the best value.
  • Miscalculating ceiling paint: Ceilings absorb more paint in the first coat. Add 10–15% extra.
  • Rounding down: Always round up — never down — when buying paint.
  • Ignoring colour batch: Paint colours vary slightly between batches. Buy all cans in one batch.
  • Mixing metric and imperial: Keep all measurements in the same unit system throughout. Our calculator handles conversions automatically.
  • Not ventilating: Paint fumes are harmful. Always paint in a well-ventilated space.
