Hanging drywall is only half the battle. The finishing stage—applying joint compound (mud), embedding tape, and sanding—is what gives a wall its smooth, seamless look. But finishing is also where material estimates go wrong most often. Order too little mud, and your project stalls mid-coat. Order too much, and you're stuck with buckets of compound that will harden before the next project.

The most common question we hear from contractors and DIYers is: "How much joint compound do I need for my drywall?" The answer depends on the total square footage, the finish level, and the tape type. This guide walks through the exact math, finish level requirements, and finishing best practices used by professional drywall finishers in the US, UK, and Australia.

1. Understanding Drywall Finish Levels

The GA-214 (Recommended Levels of Gypsum Board Finish) standard defines six finish levels (0-5). For residential work, Levels 3, 4, and 5 are the most common:

Level Coats Mud / 1,000 sq ft Use Case
Level 3 2 ~4.5 gal Garages, textured walls
Level 4 3 ~6.0 gal Standard residential (matte paint)
Level 5 3 + skim ~10.5 gal High-end, gloss paint, critical light

Important: Level 4 is the standard for most homes. If you are installing gloss or semi-gloss paint, or if the wall is in a hallway with critical lighting, you must upgrade to Level 5. The skim coat adds about 75% more mud.

2. The Math: Calculating Joint Compound

The rough formula for estimating joint compound is based on the number of coats and the total drywall area.

Formula: Total sq ft × Mud per sq ft (by level) = Total mud needed.
A standard box of joint compound contains 4.5 gallons. A 5-gallon bucket contains 5 gallons.

Example: A 1,000 sq ft drywall project at Level 4:
1,000 × 0.006 gallons per sq ft = 6 gallons.
6 gallons ÷ 4.5 gal/box = 1.33 boxes (round up to 2 boxes to account for waste).

3. Drywall Tape: Paper vs. Mesh

Tape is used to reinforce joints and corners. There are two main types:

  • Paper Tape: The professional standard. It is stronger, resists cracking better, and is used with joint compound. Paper tape is ideal for inside corners and flat seams.
  • Mesh Tape (Fiberglass): Self-adhesive. Faster to apply, but weaker. Best for small patches or areas that won't be painted with gloss. Not recommended for inside corners.

Tape Quantity: A standard roll of tape is 500 feet. For a typical 1,000 sq ft project, you need about 1.5 rolls (750 ft) to cover all seams, corners, and butt joints. Add 10% for waste.

4. Drywall Screws: The Final Count

If you are estimating finishing materials, you also need to account for screws. Standard 4x8 drywall uses about 32-36 screws per sheet. For a 1,000 sq ft project, that's about 8-10 lbs of screws. Use 1-1/4" coarse-thread screws for wood studs and 1-1/8" fine-thread screws for steel studs. Never reuse screws or use drywall screws for structural work.

5. Tools Required for Finishing

To properly finish drywall, you will need the following tools:

  • Joint Knives: 4" for tight corners, 6" for taping, 10" for finish coats, 12" for final smoothing.
  • Mud Pan: A stainless steel or plastic tray to hold the compound.
  • Corner Trowel: For applying mud to inside corners.
  • Pole Sander or Sanding Sponge: For smoothing the final coat.
  • Dust Mask: Drywall dust is a respiratory irritant—always wear protection.

6. Mud Types: All-Purpose vs. Topping

Not all joint compound is the same. The type you use depends on the coat:

  • All-Purpose: Contains a mix of gypsum and adhesive. Excellent for taping, embedding, and first coats. It dries harder than topping but is harder to sand.
  • Topping: Lighter and easier to sand. Used for the final coat. It is softer and may shrink slightly, but it produces a perfectly smooth finish.
  • Lightweight: A hybrid of the two. Good for all coats but slightly more expensive.
  • Setting-Type (Durabond): Powder mixed with water. It chemically hardens in 20-90 minutes. Best for the first coat or repairing large holes.

Pro Tip: Do not mix brands or types. Stick to one brand's all-purpose for the first two coats and the same brand's topping for the final coat.

7. Regional Drywall Standards (US vs UK vs AU)

Drywall finishing is governed by strict building codes, especially when it comes to fire-rated walls and ceilings.

United States (GA-214 & ASTM C840)

The US follows GA-214 for finish levels and ASTM C840 for installation. Fire-rated walls (like garage ceilings) must use the specified fasteners and number of coats. The US also requires moisture-resistant (green) drywall in bathrooms and kitchens.

United Kingdom (BS 8212)

The UK follows BS 8212 (Code of practice for dry lining). In the UK, the standard is Skimming, which is applying a 2-3mm layer of plaster over the entire drywall surface. This creates a harder, more impact-resistant finish than US-style taping.

Australia (AS/NZS 2589)

Australian drywall is governed by AS/NZS 2589. The key difference is the requirement for Wet Area drywall in bathrooms and laundries. Australian standards also require specific waterproofing membranes behind tiles in showers, which must be installed before the drywall finishing.

8. Cost Estimation

Finishing is the most labor-intensive part of drywall. It can account for 50-60% of the total drywall cost.

Material Costs

  • Joint Compound (US): $15 to $20 per 4.5 gal box. $18 to $25 per 5 gal bucket.
  • Joint Compound (UK): £15 to £25 per 25kg bag (finishing plaster).
  • Drywall Tape: $5 to $8 per 500 ft roll.
  • Screws: $10 to $15 per 1 lb box. $30 to $50 for a 5 lb box.

Labor Costs

Finishing labor is priced per square foot or per day:

  • US: $0.50 to $1.00 per sq ft (taping and finishing to Level 4).
  • UK: £150 to £250 per day for a plasterer (skimming).
  • Australia: AUD $10 to $20 per sq meter for set and finish.

9. Common Drywall Finishing Mistakes

  1. Skipping the Bed Coat: The first coat embeds the tape. If you skip it and try to cover the tape in one coat, the tape will bubble or peel.
  2. Overloading the First Coat: The first coat should be thin. If you put too much mud on the first coat, it will crack as it dries.
  3. Not Feathering the Edges: Each coat should be wider than the last. If you don't feather (thin) the edges, you'll see a ridge under the paint.
  4. Sanding Too Aggressively: Sanding too hard removes the paper facing on the drywall, which weakens the finish.
  5. Skipping the Primer: Drywall mud is porous. Without a PVA primer, the paint will soak in unevenly, causing visible patches.

Drywall Finishing Questions Answered

Common questions about mud, tape, screws, and finish levels.

01 How much joint compound do I need per 1,000 sq ft?
For a standard Level 4 finish, you need about 6 gallons of joint compound per 1,000 sq ft. For Level 3 (garages), use about 4.5 gallons. For Level 5 (gloss paint), you need about 10-11 gallons due to the skim coat. Use our Drywall Sheet Calculator to first determine your total square footage.
02 Should I use paper tape or mesh tape?
Paper tape is the professional standard. It is stronger, resists cracking, and is essential for inside corners. Mesh tape is self-adhesive and faster, but weaker and prone to cracking in corners or high-movement areas. For quality work, always choose paper tape. A standard roll covers 500 linear feet.
03 What is the difference between Level 4 and Level 5 drywall?
Level 4 is the standard for residential walls with flat or matte paint. It uses three coats of mud over the joints. Level 5 adds a thin skim coat over the entire wall surface. This is required for gloss or semi-gloss paint, or for walls with critical lighting (like under a window). Level 5 consumes about 75% more mud than Level 4.
04 How many screws do I need for drywall?
A standard 4x8 sheet requires about 32-36 screws for walls and ceilings. For a 1,000 sq ft project (approx. 32 sheets), you need about 8-10 lbs of screws. Use 1-1/4" coarse-thread screws for wood studs and 1-1/8" fine-thread screws for metal studs. Always buy by the box (1,000 or 5,000) for larger jobs.
05 What is the best mud for the first coat?
Use All-Purpose joint compound or Setting-Type (Durabond) for the first coat. All-purpose has strong adhesive properties for embedding tape, while setting-type chemically hardens and shrinks less. Avoid using lightweight topping for the first coat—it's too soft and won't hold tape securely.
06 How long should drywall mud dry between coats?
Drying-type compound (from a box or bucket) takes 24 to 48 hours to dry between coats, depending on humidity and temperature. Setting-type compound (Durabond, powder mixed with water) hardens in 20 to 90 minutes. Never apply the next coat before the previous one is fully dry, or you'll trap moisture and cause mold.
07 What grit sandpaper should I use for drywall?
Use 120 to 150 grit sandpaper for final smoothing. Use a sanding sponge or pole sander with light pressure. If you use too coarse a grit (like 80), you'll scratch the drywall paper and weaken the surface. If you use too fine a grit (like 220), you'll clog the sandpaper. For Level 5 finishes, use 150-180 grit.
