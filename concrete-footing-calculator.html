Understanding Concrete Footing Calculations

Concrete footings are the unsung heroes of every structural project. Whether you are building a residential home, a commercial extension, or a simple backyard deck, the footing is the critical interface between the structure above and the soil below. Its primary purpose is to transfer the load of the building safely into the ground, preventing settlement, shifting, and structural failure.

Because footings are buried underground, mistakes are incredibly expensive to fix. Over-ordering concrete leads to wasted money and the logistical nightmare of disposing of excess ready-mix. Under-ordering means a halted pour, cold joints, and potential structural weakness. That is why using a precise concrete footing calculator is not just a convenience—it is a mandatory step in the pre-construction phase.

At BuildMetric, our tool is engineered for US (IRC), UK (BS 8500), and Australian (AS 2870) standards, ensuring that whether you are pouring a simple garden wall footing in London or a heavy garage pad in Texas, your volume calculations are exact.

The Core Formula: How to Calculate Footing Volume

Regardless of the complexity of the project, the foundation of all concrete footing math is the volume formula:

Volume = Length × Width × Depth

While the formula seems simple, the complexity arises in unit conversion. Concrete is typically ordered in Cubic Yards (cu yd) in the US, and Cubic Meters (m³) in the UK and Australia.

Imperial Math (US)

In the US, dimensions are usually taken in feet and inches. To find cubic yards, you must convert all measurements to feet, multiply them, and then divide by 27 (since there are 27 cubic feet in a cubic yard).

  • Convert inches to feet: Depth in inches ÷ 12
  • Calculate Cubic Feet: Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft)
  • Convert to Cubic Yards: Total Cubic Feet ÷ 27

Metric Math (UK & AU)

In metric systems, dimensions are often mixed (meters for length, millimeters for depth). It is easiest to convert everything to meters before calculating.

  • Convert mm to meters: Depth in mm ÷ 1000
  • Calculate Cubic Meters: Length (m) × Width (m) × Depth (m)

Strip Footings vs. Isolated Pad Footings

Our calculator allows you to toggle between the two most common footing types. It is critical to know which one you are pouring, as the math differs slightly based on the width input.

Continuous Strip Footings

Used to support continuous loads like load-bearing walls (masonry or timber). These are poured in long trenches. The width is typically calculated in inches (e.g., a 16" wide trench) while the length is measured in linear feet.

Isolated Pad Footings

Used to support concentrated point loads, such as timber posts, steel columns, or deck piers. These are square or rectangular pads. The width is usually measured in feet (e.g., a 2ft x 2ft pad) or meters in metric.

Why Your Footing Needs a Waste Allowance

If the math says you need 4.0 cubic yards, you should never order exactly 4.0 cubic yards. Real-world trenching is rarely perfectly uniform. The soil may cave slightly, the sub-grade might be uneven, or some concrete will be lost during the pump transfer.

Our tool defaults to a 5% waste allowance, but for irregular hand-dug trenches, 10% is highly recommended. For a deeper dive into this topic, read our guide on Concrete Waste Allowances to ensure you never run short on pour day.

Building Codes: IRC (US), BS (UK), and NCC (AU)

Concrete volume is only half the battle. The width and depth of your footing must be sufficient to handle the structural load and the soil's bearing capacity. If you build a footing that is too narrow, it will sink. If it is too shallow, it will heave during frost. Here is how the three major regions handle footing requirements.

1. USA: IRC Table R403.1

The International Residential Code (IRC) mandates minimum footing widths based on the soil's bearing capacity and the number of floors supported. Here is a quick reference table for a typical one-story build (assuming a minimum 6-inch thickness):

Soil Bearing Capacity Min. Footing Width (1-Story) Min. Footing Width (2-Story)
1,500 psf (Soft Clay) 12 inches 15 inches
2,000 psf (Sandy Soil) 10 inches 12 inches
3,000 psf (Gravel) 8 inches 10 inches

2. UK: BS 8004 & BS 8500

In the UK, traditional strip foundations are typically governed by BS 8004. The width depends on the total load and the allowable bearing pressure of the subsoil. For domestic extensions, a common rule of thumb is a trench at least 450mm (18 inches) wide, poured to a minimum depth of 225mm (9 inches). For low-rise buildings on firm clay, "Trench Fill" is common, where the trench is filled almost entirely with concrete up to the DPC (Damp Proof Course) level.

3. Australia: AS 2870

Australian footing design is heavily dictated by reactive clay soils, governed by AS 2870. Sites are classified from Class A (sand/rock) to Class P (problem sites). On highly reactive Class H or E soils, footings must be deeper and often require stiffened raft slabs with edge beams, rather than simple strip footings, to prevent cracking as the soil expands and contracts with moisture.

Structural Integrity: Rebar in Footings

Concrete is incredibly strong in compression, but it has almost zero tensile strength. This means if the ground shifts or settles unevenly, an unreinforced concrete footing will crack and snap. To prevent this, steel reinforcement (rebar) is mandatory.

For standard residential strip footings, a single layer of rebar (often #4 / 12mm) is placed near the bottom of the trench, suspended on plastic chairs so it sits in the middle of the concrete pour. For isolated pad footings, a grid of rebar is used. To calculate exact steel requirements, lap splices, and chair spacing, use our dedicated Rebar Calculator.

Real-World Case Study: 20x30 Detached Garage Footing

Project: 600 sq ft Detached Garage (Ohio, USA)

The Spec: The client needed a continuous strip footing for a 20 ft by 30 ft detached garage. The soil was tested at a bearing capacity of 2,000 psf. Local frost depth required the footing to be poured 36 inches deep (to prevent frost heave).

The Math: Based on IRC Table R403.1 for 2,000 psf soil, the minimum width is 10 inches. However, the contractor chose a standard 12-inch (1 ft) wide trench for easier rebar installation and a 8-inch thick pour.

Total Linear Feet = (20+20+30+30) = 100 ft
Width = 12 inches (1 ft)
Depth = 8 inches (0.66 ft)
Volume = 100 × 1 × 0.66 = 66 cu ft
With 10% waste = 72.6 cu ft
Final Order = 2.68 Cubic Yards (Ordered 3.0 cu yd)

The Lesson: By calculating the exact volume and adding a 10% buffer for the uneven hand-dug trench, the contractor ordered 3 cubic yards. The pour took exactly 2.8 cubic yards, leaving just enough for a small pad at the garage door threshold without any leftover concrete to dispose of.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much concrete do I need for a 16x20 garage footing?
For a 16x20 garage, the perimeter is 72 linear feet. Assuming a standard 12-inch wide and 8-inch deep strip footing, the volume is 48 cubic feet. With a 10% waste allowance, you will need approximately 2 cubic yards of ready-mix concrete.
What is the minimum width of a concrete footing?
According to the International Residential Code (IRC R403.1), the minimum width for a footing is typically 12 inches for one-story buildings on soft clay (1,500 psf soil). On stronger soil like gravel (3,000 psf), the minimum width can drop to 8 inches.
Do I need rebar in my concrete footings?
Yes, almost all modern building codes require steel reinforcement in footings. While concrete is strong in compression, rebar provides the necessary tensile strength to prevent cracking when the ground shifts. Use our Rebar Calculator to determine exact lengths and lap splices.
How many 80lb bags of concrete are in a cubic yard?
It takes exactly 45 bags of 80lb concrete mix to equal one cubic yard. For large footings, ordering ready-mix by the truck is much more cost-effective and faster than mixing 45 bags per yard by hand. See our Concrete Bag Calculator for precise bag counts.
What is the difference between a strip footing and a pad footing?
A strip footing is a continuous concrete pour used to support load-bearing walls. A pad footing is an isolated, square/rectangular concrete block used to support point loads like deck posts or steel columns. Our calculator allows you to switch between both types.
How deep should a concrete footing be for frost?
The footing must be poured below the local frost line to prevent heaving. In the US, frost depths range from 0 inches in the deep south to over 60 inches in northern states. In the UK (BS 8500), typical domestic strip footings are poured at least 450mm (18 inches) deep.
How much waste allowance should I add to my footing concrete order?
We recommend a 5% waste allowance for machine-dug trenches and 10% for hand-dug trenches. The soil can cave slightly, or the trench floor might be uneven. It is always better to have a small amount of leftover concrete than to run short mid-pour. Read our guide on Waste Allowances.
