Painting is often perceived as the easiest part of a construction or renovation project. However, professional estimators know that paint is one of the most common areas for budget overruns. Ordering too little results in costly delays and mismatched dye lots; ordering too much wastes money on paint that will eventually dry out in the can.

To achieve a flawless finish, you must look beyond the basic Length × Width formula. Paint is a complex chemical emulsion, and its coverage rate is heavily influenced by the surface you are painting, the tools you use, and the environmental conditions. This guide breaks down the exact methodology used by professional painters and quantity surveyors across the US, UK, and Australia.

The Science of Paint Coverage: Why Labels Lie

If you look at a standard can of paint, you will see a coverage rate—typically around 350 to 400 square feet per gallon (8.6 to 9.8 square meters per liter). This number is derived from a laboratory test performed under perfect conditions: a perfectly smooth, non-porous surface, applied at a specific wet-film thickness, using a standardized brush.

In the real world, conditions are rarely perfect. If you are painting bare drywall (plasterboard), the gypsum will act like a sponge and absorb a significant amount of the first coat. If you are painting rough exterior stucco, the deep texture means you have much more surface area than the flat dimensions suggest. This is why our calculator uses different coverage baselines depending on your paint and surface type.

Understanding Hiding Power and Sheen

The "hiding power" of a paint—its ability to completely obscure the color underneath—is dictated by the ratio of pigment to binder.

  • Primers and High-Hiding Paints: These contain high levels of titanium dioxide (the primary white pigment) and have lower binder levels. They cover well but often require a topcoat for durability. Coverage is usually lower (around 250-300 sq ft/gal) because the film build is thicker.
  • Sheen Levels: Matte (flat) paints have a higher pigment volume concentration (PVC), making them thicker and harder to spread, resulting in lower coverage. Gloss or semi-gloss paints have more binder and less pigment, spreading thinner and often covering more area per gallon. However, gloss paints highlight surface imperfections, meaning you must spend more time on surface preparation.

Surface Porosity: The Hidden Variable

The porosity of your substrate is arguably the most critical factor in paint estimation. When estimating for a full home renovation or new build, you must account for the following:

1. New Drywall and Plasterboard

New drywall is highly porous. The paper facing and the joint compound (mud) absorb moisture rapidly. If you apply a standard topcoat directly to new drywall, it will flash-dry too quickly, leading to uneven sheen, lap marks, and poor adhesion. You must apply a dedicated drywall primer (often called a "PVA primer").

Estimator's Rule: For new drywall, budget 1 coat of primer at 300 sq ft/gal, followed by 2 coats of topcoat. For the topcoat, the first coat will still be partially absorbed, so calculate it at 350 sq ft/gal.

2. Previously Painted Surfaces

If the wall is already painted with a similar color, the surface is sealed. You can expect maximum coverage from your paint. However, if the previous paint is a dark color and you are painting it white, you may need 3 coats, or a high-hiding primer. Always wash the walls with a degreaser (like TSP in the US) before painting; kitchen grease and bathroom soap scum act as a barrier, preventing paint adhesion and reducing coverage.

3. Bare Wood and Metal

Bare wood is highly absorbent, especially end-grain. It requires a wood primer or a specialized stain-blocking primer. Knots in pine will bleed sap through latex paint, requiring a shellac-based primer (e.g., BIN) on the knots. For bare steel or iron, a rust-inhibitive metal primer is mandatory before applying a topcoat.

Regional Standards: US vs UK vs Australia

Different regions have different codes and standards for paint application, which can impact material selection, environmental regulations (VOCs), and required coats.

United States (MPI & ASTM)

In the US, the Master Painters Institute (MPI) sets the gold standard for commercial and residential paint performance. They categorize paints by gloss level, substrate, and chemical composition. The US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) heavily regulates Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs). For interior paints, look for paints with VOC levels below 50 g/L (Low-VOC) or 5 g/L (Zero-VOC) to comply with modern green building standards like LEED.

United Kingdom (BS 6150 & BS EN 13300)

The UK adheres to BS 6150 (Code of practice for painting of buildings). This standard dictates surface preparation, application methods, and drying times based on the substrate. Additionally, BS EN 13300 is used to classify water-borne paints. It rates paints on wet scrub resistance (Class 1 to 5) and opacity (hiding power). When estimating for UK projects, remember that older houses may have lime plaster, which requires breathable mineral paints (like clay or limewash) rather than modern acrylics.

Australia (AS/NZS 2311)

Australian construction follows AS/NZS 2311 (Guide to the painting of buildings). The Australian climate is uniquely harsh, with extreme UV exposure and high humidity in coastal areas. AS/NZS 2311 mandates the use of 3-coat systems (primer, intermediate, topcoat) for most exterior surfaces to ensure maximum durability against UV degradation and mould. For coastal areas (within 1km of the shoreline), specific marine-grade coatings and stainless steel fixings are often required.

How to Measure Complex Rooms

Standard rectangular rooms are easy to calculate, but real estate is rarely perfectly square. Here is how professional estimators handle complex geometries:

Sloped and Vaulted Ceilings

If a wall has a sloped ceiling (like in an attic or under a staircase), you cannot simply multiply the length by the highest point. Instead, divide the wall into geometric shapes. Break it into a rectangle (the main body of the wall) and a triangle (the sloped section). The area of a triangle is (Base × Height) ÷ 2. Add these two areas together to get the total paintable square footage.

Bay Windows and Alcoves

Measure each individual flat plane of the bay window, including the small return walls and the ceiling/soffit above the window if it is being painted. Do not subtract the window glass from the wall area if you are using a calculator for the whole room; instead, use our built-in "Deduct Windows/Doors" feature to subtract a standard area (e.g., 15 sq ft for a window) per opening.

Open Floor Plans and Multi-Level Spaces

For a two-story foyer or an open-plan living room, measure the full height from the floor to the highest ceiling. Remember that painting a tall wall often requires scaffolding or extension ladders, which significantly increases the labor cost. If you are hiring a pro, expect to pay a premium for "high-wall" painting.

The "80% Rule" of Surface Preparation

Professional painters often say that 80% of a paint job is in the preparation. If you skip prep, even the most expensive paint will peel, blister, or show every imperfection. Here is the standard prep routine:

  • Cleaning: Wash walls with a degreaser (like TSP or sugar soap in the UK). Grease from kitchens and soap scum from bathrooms will prevent paint from adhering.
  • Patching and Sanding: Fill nail holes and cracks with spackle (polyfilla in the UK/AU). Once dry, sand flush with 120-grit sandpaper. Sand the entire wall lightly to create a "mechanical key" (micro-scratches) so the new paint can grip. Wipe away all dust with a tack cloth.
  • Caulking: Use a paintable acrylic caulk to seal the gaps between the wall and the baseboards, window frames, and door casings. This creates a professional, seamless look.
  • Taping: Apply high-quality painter's tape to protect trim, glass, and floors. Use a utility knife to score the tape edge before peeling it off to prevent the paint from tearing.

Cost Estimation: Materials vs. Labor

If you are budgeting a painting project for a client, your estimate must cover labor, materials, and overhead.

Material Costs: A gallon of premium interior paint costs anywhere from $35 to $80 in the US, £25 to £60 in the UK, and AUD $50 to $120 in Australia. Do not forget to factor in the cost of primer, sandpaper, tape, caulk, and brushes/rollers (which are consumables).

Labor Costs: Labor is highly regional. In the US, painters typically charge $2.00 to $4.00 per square foot of wall area. In the UK, a painter might charge £150 to £250 per day, and in Australia, expect AUD $40 to $70 per hour.

A quick rule of thumb for a standard interior repaint: Estimate the total paintable area and multiply by $3.50 (or the local equivalent) to get a rough total cost. Remember to add 15-20% to your final estimate as a contingency for unforeseen issues (like dry rot or wallpaper removal).

Common Painting Mistakes to Avoid

Even seasoned DIYers make these mistakes. Avoiding them will save you time, money, and frustration:

  1. Not using a primer: Skipping primer on bare drywall or wood is a recipe for disaster. The wood tannins will bleed through, and the drywall will absorb your expensive topcoat. Always use a primer.
  2. Over-thinning the paint: Do not add water or paint thinner to the can unless the manufacturer explicitly instructs you to do so. Thinning the paint reduces its durability and hiding power. If the paint is too thick, stir it thoroughly—don't dilute it.
  3. Painting over dirt: Paint over dust or grease will inevitably peel. Always clean and degrease surfaces before painting.
  4. Using the wrong roller nap: A 3/8-inch nap roller is perfect for smooth interior walls. If you are painting exterior stucco or rough brick, you need a 3/4-inch or 1-inch nap roller to get the paint into the deep crevices.
  5. Painting in direct sunlight (Exterior): Painting exterior walls when the surface is in direct sunlight causes the paint to dry too quickly, leading to blisters and poor adhesion. Always paint the side of the house that is in the shade, or paint early in the morning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about estimating paint quantities and coverage.

How much paint do I need for a standard 12x12 room?
A standard 12x12 room with 8-foot ceilings has roughly 384 square feet of wall area. After deducting 1 door and 2 windows, the net paintable area is about 330 square feet. For two coats of standard interior paint (assuming 350 sq ft coverage per gallon), you will need roughly 2 gallons of paint. If you are painting the ceiling as well (144 sq ft), add another half gallon.
Do I need 2 or 3 coats of paint?
For a standard repaint where the new color is similar to the old one, 2 coats are usually sufficient. However, if you are painting a light color over a dark color (e.g., white over red), or painting bare drywall for the first time, you will likely need a primer coat plus 3 coats of topcoat to achieve full opacity and a uniform finish.
How much does 1 gallon or 1 litre of paint cover?
In the US, 1 gallon of standard interior paint typically covers 350 to 400 square feet on a smooth, primed surface. In the UK and Australia, 1 litre of paint covers roughly 8 to 10 square meters. Note that rough surfaces like stucco or brick will drastically reduce this coverage, sometimes by up to 50%.
Does a primer count as one of the coats?
Technically, primer is a coat, but it is not a topcoat. Primer is formulated to adhere to the bare surface and seal its porosity, providing a uniform base for the topcoat. It does not provide the durability or washability of a topcoat. Therefore, a standard paint job consists of 1 coat of primer followed by 2 coats of topcoat.
Do I need to subtract windows and doors from my calculation?
Yes. Our calculator automatically subtracts a standard area for doors and windows to give you the "net paintable area." If you do not subtract them, you will overestimate the paint needed and likely end up with extra cans. However, it is always wise to keep a small amount of leftover paint (about a quart or 1 litre) for future touch-ups.
Can I use interior paint on exterior walls?
No, never use interior paint on exterior walls. Interior paints are not formulated to withstand UV radiation, rain, or extreme temperature fluctuations. They will crack, peel, and fade within a year. Exterior paints contain special mildewcides and UV blockers, and they remain flexible to accommodate the expansion and contraction of the exterior substrate.
How do I calculate paint for ceilings and trim?
Ceilings: Calculate the length × width of the floor (which is the same as the ceiling area). Ceilings usually require a flat (matte) paint, which covers well, so use the standard 350 sq ft/gal coverage.

Trim (baseboards, casings, doors): Trim is usually painted with a semi-gloss or gloss enamel. Because trim is narrow and has multiple sides, it is difficult to measure accurately by area. A good rule of thumb is to estimate about 1 gallon of trim paint for every 1,000 square feet of floor area in the house.
