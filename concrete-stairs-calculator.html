Pouring concrete stairs is one of the most complex concrete projects a DIYer or contractor can undertake. Unlike a flat slab or a simple footing, stairs require precise formwork, careful volume calculation, and proper reinforcement. If you get the math wrong, you'll either run out of concrete halfway through the pour (causing a cold joint that ruins the stairs) or waste hundreds of dollars on excess material.

The most common question we hear is: "How many yards of concrete do I need for my stairs?" The answer depends on the number of steps, the dimensions of each step, and whether you are pouring a landing. This guide walks through the exact math, formwork considerations, and the reinforcement needed to build stairs that will last for decades.

1. The Math: Calculating Stair Volume

Concrete stairs are essentially a series of stacked rectangular blocks. To calculate the total volume, you calculate the volume of each step and add them together.

Volume of one step (cubic ft) = Rise (ft) × Run (ft) × Width (ft)

However, this simple method can be misleading. A real concrete stair is typically poured as a solid shape, not as individual blocks. The best approach is to break the stairs into two components:

  • The Steps: Multiply the average height of the steps (Rise × Number of Steps ÷ 2) by the total Run (Run × Number of Steps) and the Width.
  • The Base Slab (Waist): The staircase is supported by a sloped slab underneath. Calculate this as a rectangle using the hypotenuse of the rise and run (using Pythagorean theorem).

Estimator's Rule: Always add a 10% waste factor. Concrete can spill during the pour, and the uneven ground beneath the formwork may require more material than calculated.

2. Formwork: The Key to a Good Stair

The formwork (the wooden mold that holds the concrete while it cures) is the most critical part of a stair pour. If the formwork is not strong enough, it will bulge or blow out under the weight of the wet concrete.

Materials: Use 3/4-inch plywood for the sides (stringers) and 2x4 or 2x6 lumber for the risers. The riser boards must be cut to the exact height of each step.

Bracing: The formwork must be braced heavily from the outside. Use stakes driven into the ground and cross-bracing to prevent the sides from bowing outward under pressure.

Release Agent: Coat the inside of the formwork with a concrete release agent (or motor oil in a pinch). This ensures the wood pulls away cleanly when you strip the formwork after curing.

3. Rebar and Reinforcement

Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. A staircase is subject to both, especially at the bottom of the steps. If the stairs are attached to a structure (like a house or a pier), you must use rebar.

  • Rebar Size: Use #3 (3/8") or #4 (1/2") rebar. For most residential stairs, #3 is sufficient.
  • Grid Pattern: Lay rebar in a grid pattern about 2 inches from the bottom of the formwork. Use a "chairs" (small concrete blocks) to keep the rebar off the wood.
  • Tying: Tie the rebar intersections with wire. This prevents the grid from moving during the pour.

Important: If your stairs are free-standing (not attached to a structure), the reinforcement is even more critical. Free-standing stairs need a thicker base slab and heavier rebar.

4. Pouring and Curing

The concrete pour must be continuous. If you stop halfway through, you create a "cold joint" between the two layers, which weakens the structure and creates a visible line.

  1. Start at the bottom: Always pour from the bottom step up. This prevents the concrete from sliding down and accumulating at the bottom.
  2. Vibrate: Use a concrete vibrator (or tap the formwork with a hammer) to remove air bubbles. Air pockets weaken the concrete and create a rough surface.
  3. Level each step: Use a trowel to level each step as you pour. Make sure each step has a slight slope (usually 1/8 inch) toward the front so water runs off.
  4. Cure: Cover the stairs with plastic sheeting for the first 24 hours to retain moisture. You can strip the formwork after 24-48 hours, but wait at least 7 days before walking on the stairs.

5. Bagged Concrete vs Ready-Mix

For stair projects, you have two options: mix your own concrete from 80lb bags, or order ready-mix concrete from a truck.

Bagged Concrete (DIY)

Best for small staircases (3-4 steps). You will need a concrete mixer and a lot of water. Each 80lb bag yields about 0.60 cubic feet of concrete. For a 3-step staircase (approx 10 cubic feet), you need about 17 bags.
Pro Tip: Use our Concrete Calculator to find the exact number of bags needed.

Ready-Mix Concrete (Professional)

Best for larger staircases (5+ steps) or if you want a perfect finish. Ready-mix trucks have a minimum order (usually 1 cubic yard). They deliver the concrete directly to your formwork. You must have 2-3 helpers to move the concrete quickly before it sets.

6. Regional Codes for Concrete Stairs

United States (IRC)

The International Residential Code (IRC) dictates stair dimensions. The maximum riser height is 7.75 inches, and the minimum tread depth is 10 inches. Stairs must have a minimum headroom of 6 feet 8 inches. For exterior stairs (like those to a deck), the stairs must be supported on footings dug below the frost line.

United Kingdom (BS 5395)

The UK follows BS 5395. The maximum riser height is usually 220mm (8.6 inches), and the minimum tread is 220mm. External stairs in the UK often require anti-slip finishes (like brush-finished concrete) to prevent accidents in wet weather.

Australia (NCC)

Australian standards are governed by the NCC. Riser height must be between 115mm and 190mm, and tread depth between 250mm and 355mm. External stairs in Australia must be designed to withstand wind loads and termite protection (if timber formwork is left in place).

7. Cost Estimation

Concrete stairs are a significant investment, but they are incredibly durable.

Material Costs

  • Bagged Concrete (US): $5 to $8 per 80lb bag. (A small stair may need 20-30 bags).
  • Ready-Mix (US): $150 to $200 per cubic yard (plus delivery fees).
  • UK Concrete: £100 to £150 per cubic meter (ready-mix).
  • AU Concrete: AUD $200 to $300 per cubic meter.
  • Rebar & Formwork: Add $50 to $150 for plywood and rebar.

Labor Costs

Building concrete stairs is highly skilled labor. If you hire a professional:

  • US: $50 to $100 per square foot of step surface (installed).
  • UK: £500 to £1,500 per staircase.
  • Australia: AUD $800 to $2,000 per staircase.

8. Common Concrete Stair Mistakes

  1. Weak Formwork: If the formwork bows or collapses, the stairs will be uneven or unwalkable. Use plenty of bracing.
  2. No Rebar: Concrete stairs without reinforcement will crack at the base of the steps over time.
  3. Pouring Too Slow: If the concrete starts to set before you finish pouring, you will get a cold joint. Have a team ready to move fast.
  4. Not Sloping the Steps: Water pooling on a concrete step will freeze in winter, creating a slip hazard. Always slope each step 1/8 inch toward the front.
  5. Stripping the Formwork Too Early: Waiting at least 24 hours before removing the formwork is critical. Concrete continues to cure for 28 days.

Concrete Stairs Questions Answered

Common questions about concrete volume, formwork, and stair design.

01 How many yards of concrete do I need for a 4-step staircase?
A standard 4-step staircase (3 feet wide, 7" rise, 10" run) requires approximately 0.5 to 0.7 cubic yards of concrete. This is about 13 to 18 cubic feet. You can use our Concrete Calculator to calculate the exact volume for your specific dimensions.
02 Do I need rebar for concrete stairs?
Yes, always use rebar. Concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. Stairs experience tension forces (especially where the steps meet the base slab) that can cause cracking. Use a grid of #3 (3/8") or #4 (1/2") rebar, placed 2 inches from the bottom of the formwork. Check our Rebar Calculator for precise quantities.
03 What is the ideal rise and run for concrete stairs?
For comfortable walking, the rise plus run should equal roughly 17 to 18 inches. A standard residential step has a rise of 7 inches and a run of 10 to 11 inches. For exterior stairs, wider treads (12 inches or more) are recommended for safety in wet weather. Use our Stair Rise/Run Calculator to verify your dimensions against IRC code.
04 How long does concrete take to cure for stairs?
Concrete sets in 24-48 hours (formwork can be removed), but it fully cures in 28 days. You can walk on the stairs after 3-4 days, but avoid heavy loads (like moving furniture) for at least a week. For the first 7 days, keep the concrete moist by spraying it with water daily to prevent cracking.
05 Can I build concrete stairs on dirt?
No. Concrete stairs must be built on a solid foundation. The base must be excavated, leveled, and compacted. You need a layer of gravel (4-6 inches) and possibly a concrete footing underneath, especially if the stairs are free-standing or built on a slope. Building stairs directly on dirt will cause them to settle and crack.
06 How do I prevent my concrete stairs from cracking?
Use proper reinforcement (rebar grid). Ensure the sub-base is compacted. Add control joints (small grooves cut into the concrete) to direct cracks to a specific location. Keep the concrete moist during curing. In cold climates, use air-entrained concrete to prevent freeze-thaw damage.
07 Can I use bagged concrete for a staircase?
Yes, for small staircases. If you are building 3-4 steps, 80lb bags mixed in a wheelbarrow or mixer are sufficient. However, you must mix and pour quickly to avoid cold joints. For larger staircases (5+ steps), ready-mix concrete is strongly recommended because it is faster, stronger, and produces a more consistent finish.
