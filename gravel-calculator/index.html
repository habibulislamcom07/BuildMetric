What Is a Gravel Calculator?

A gravel calculator is a tool that estimates how much gravel you need for a project — measured in volume (cubic feet, cubic yards, cubic metres), weight (pounds, kilograms, tons, tonnes) or the number of standard bags. Instead of guessing and ending up with too little gravel (which means a trip back to the supplier and a delay in your project) or too much (which means wasted money sitting in your driveway), you enter your project's dimensions and the calculator works out the exact quantity.

Gravel quantity depends on several factors: the shape of the area you are covering (rectangular, circular or irregular), the dimensions, the depth of the gravel layer, the type of gravel you are using (pea gravel, crushed stone, river rock and so on — each has a different density), and the waste allowance for spillage and compaction. This calculator takes all of these into account so you get a reliable estimate before you buy.

Gravel is one of the most widely used landscaping and construction materials in the world. It is used in driveways, walkways, patios, garden beds, drainage systems, French drains, playgrounds, decorative landscaping and as a base layer under concrete and pavers. Whether you are building a new gravel driveway, refreshing an old one, or adding decorative gravel to a garden bed, accurate gravel estimation saves time, money and frustration.

Whether you are building a gravel driveway in Texas, refreshing a garden path in London, installing a French drain in Sydney, or prepping a patio base in Toronto, this calculator gives you an accurate estimate in both imperial and metric units.

How to Use This Gravel Calculator

Using this gravel calculator is straightforward. Follow these six steps:

  1. Choose the shape. Most projects use a rectangular or square area (driveway, walkway, patio base). For a round patio, fire pit or circular garden bed, choose the circular option. If you already know the total square footage, choose the area-based option.
  2. Enter the dimensions. For a rectangular area, provide length, width and depth. For a circular area, provide diameter and depth. For an area-based calculation, provide total area and depth. Use the same units shown on each field.
  3. Select the gravel type. Different gravel types have different densities. Crushed stone weighs about 100 lb per cubic foot (1,600 kg/m³). Pea gravel is lighter at 96 lb/ft³. River rock is 95 lb/ft³. Decomposed granite is 105 lb/ft³. Crusher run is heaviest at 110 lb/ft³. Choose the closest match to your supply.
  4. Set a waste allowance. We default to 10% for standard conditions. Use 5% for simple rectangular areas and 15% for irregular shapes or rough bases. Gravel also compacts by 15–20% when properly tamped, so it is better to slightly overestimate.
  5. Choose your unit system. Use the toggle at the top to switch between Imperial (feet, inches, pounds, tons) and Metric (metres, centimetres, kilograms, tonnes). Your preference is saved for next time.
  6. Click Calculate. The result shows volume in cubic feet, cubic yards and cubic metres, plus weight in pounds, kilograms, US tons and metric tonnes — and the number of standard bags needed.

Pro Tip: Always order 10% extra gravel beyond the calculated quantity. Gravel compacts significantly when tamped, spills during transport, and can be wasted due to uneven subgrade. Having spares on hand means you can finish the project without a trip back to the supplier.

Formula Used by This Calculator

All calculations are performed internally in metric units, then converted back to your preferred unit system for display. The following formulas are used:

Step 1 — Rectangular Volume

Volume = Length × Width × Depth All measurements must be in the same unit.
Depth is usually given in inches or centimetres — convert to feet or metres first.

Step 2 — Circular Volume

Volume = π × r² × Depth Where r = radius (diameter ÷ 2).
π (pi) ≈ 3.14159.

Step 3 — Area-Based Volume

Volume = Area × Depth Use when you already know the total square footage or square metres.

Step 4 — Weight Calculation

Weight = Volume × Density Crushed stone: 100 lb/ft³ (1,600 kg/m³)
Pea gravel: 96 lb/ft³ (1,540 kg/m³)
River rock: 95 lb/ft³ (1,520 kg/m³)
Decomposed granite: 105 lb/ft³ (1,680 kg/m³)
Crusher run: 110 lb/ft³ (1,760 kg/m³)
Washed gravel: 98 lb/ft³ (1,570 kg/m³)

Step 5 — Apply Waste Allowance

Final Volume = Volume × (1 + Waste% ÷ 100) Standard recommendation: 10%.

Step 6 — Volume Unit Conversions

Cubic Yards = Cubic Feet ÷ 27 Cubic Metres = Cubic Feet × 0.0283168 US Tons = Weight (lb) ÷ 2,000 Metric Tonnes = Weight (kg) ÷ 1,000

Step 7 — Bags of Gravel

Bags = Weight ÷ Bag Weight 50 lb bag ≈ 0.5 cu ft (0.014 m³)
25 kg bag ≈ 0.015 m³

Understanding the Variables

  • Volume: The total space the gravel will occupy, in cubic units.
  • Depth: The thickness of the gravel layer you are installing.
  • Density: The weight of gravel per unit volume, which varies by type and moisture.
  • Waste %: Extra material for spillage, compaction and uneven subgrade.
  • Bag Weight: The weight of gravel in one retail bag (50 lb or 25 kg standard).

Why Round Up, Not Down?

Gravel calculations involve rounding in two places: volume and bag count. Always round up, never down. If your calculation gives 6.2 cubic yards, order 6.5 or 7 cubic yards. If it gives 32.4 bags, order 33. Running short mid-project is far more expensive than having spare gravel at the end — especially if you have already opened the bags and cannot return them.

Gravel Compaction

One of the most overlooked aspects of gravel estimation is compaction. Freshly poured gravel is loose and contains voids. When properly tamped or vibrated, gravel compacts by 15–20%. This means the volume you install will occupy less space than the volume you order. Always add a waste allowance for compaction, especially for driveway and patio base layers. Wet gravel is also denser than dry gravel, so the same volume weighs more.

Worked Example

Let us work through three examples — a residential gravel driveway, a circular patio base, and a garden path using area-based calculation.

Example 1 — Residential Driveway (10 ft × 20 ft, 4 in deep, crushed stone, 10% waste)

  1. Convert depth to feet: 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft
  2. Volume: 10 × 20 × 0.333 = 66.67 cu ft
  3. Apply 10% waste: 66.67 × 1.10 = 73.33 cu ft
  4. Cubic yards: 73.33 ÷ 27 = 2.72 cu yd
  5. Weight (crushed stone, 100 lb/ft³): 73.33 × 100 = 7,333 lb
  6. US tons: 7,333 ÷ 2,000 = 3.67 tons
  7. Bags (50 lb each): 7,333 ÷ 50 = 147 bags

Result: You need about 2.72 cubic yards of crushed stone (73.33 cubic feet), weighing approximately 7,333 lb — or 3.67 US tons. For bulk order, get 3 cubic yards.

Example 2 — Circular Patio Base (12 ft diameter, 4 in deep, crusher run, 10% waste)

  1. Radius: 12 ÷ 2 = 6 ft
  2. Depth in feet: 4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft
  3. Volume: π × 6² × 0.333 = 37.7 cu ft
  4. Apply 10% waste: 37.7 × 1.10 = 41.47 cu ft
  5. Cubic yards: 41.47 ÷ 27 = 1.54 cu yd
  6. Weight (crusher run, 110 lb/ft³): 41.47 × 110 = 4,562 lb
  7. US tons: 4,562 ÷ 2,000 = 2.28 tons

Result: You need about 1.54 cubic yards of crusher run, weighing approximately 4,562 lb — or 2.28 US tons.

Example 3 — Garden Path (Area-based: 30 m², 5 cm deep, pea gravel, 5% waste)

  1. Depth in metres: 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05 m
  2. Volume: 30 × 0.05 = 1.5 m³
  3. Apply 5% waste: 1.5 × 1.05 = 1.575 m³
  4. Weight (pea gravel, 1,540 kg/m³): 1.575 × 1,540 = 2,426 kg
  5. Metric tonnes: 2,426 ÷ 1,000 = 2.43 tonnes
  6. Bags (25 kg each): 2,426 ÷ 25 = 98 bags

Result: You need about 1.575 cubic metres of pea gravel, weighing approximately 2,426 kg — or 2.43 metric tonnes. For bulk order, get 1.6 cubic metres.

Pro Tip: For driveways, install gravel in two layers — a 4-inch base layer of larger crushed stone (1.5–2 inch), then a 4-inch top layer of smaller gravel (3/4 inch). This two-layer approach provides better drainage, stability and appearance than a single thick layer. For patios and walkways, use a single 2–4 inch layer of your chosen gravel.

Types of Gravel

Not all gravel is the same. Different types are designed for different applications:

  • Crushed Stone: Angular, machine-crushed rock. Interlocks and stays in place. Best for driveways. Sizes range from 3/4 inch to 2 inches.
  • Pea Gravel: Small, rounded stones (typically 1/8 to 3/8 inch). Smooth texture. Used for walkways, garden beds and playgrounds. Shifts under tires — not ideal for driveways.
  • River Rock: Larger rounded stones (typically 1–3 inches). Smooth, decorative. Used for landscaping, dry creek beds and decorative features.
  • Decomposed Granite (DG): Crushed granite with a fine, sandy texture. Compacts firmly. Used for walkways, patios and xeriscaping.
  • Crusher Run: Crushed stone mixed with stone dust (fines). Compacts into a firm, semi-solid surface. Best for driveway base layers and construction entrances.
  • Washed Gravel: Clean, screened gravel without fines. Drains freely. Used for drainage, French drains, septic systems and concrete mixes.
  • Bank Run Gravel: Unprocessed gravel straight from the pit. Contains sand, silt and clay. Cheapest option, but not suitable for exposed surfaces.
  • Pea Stone / Bean Gravel: Similar to pea gravel but slightly larger. Used for decorative landscaping and garden features.
  • Lava Rock: Porous volcanic rock. Lightweight and colourful. Used for decorative landscaping and xeriscaping.
  • Recycled Concrete: Crushed concrete from demolition. Eco-friendly alternative to natural gravel. Used for driveways, base layers and drainage.

Industry Standards and Codes

Gravel used in construction and landscaping must comply with several industry standards and specifications:

  • ASTM C33: Standard Specification for Concrete Aggregates (USA)
  • ASTM D2940: Standard Specification for Graded Aggregate Material for Bases or Subbases for Highways or Airports
  • ASTM D448: Standard Classification for Sizes of Aggregate for Road and Bridge Construction
  • BS EN 13242: Aggregates for Unbound and Hydraulically Bound Materials (UK / Europe)
  • BS EN 12620: Aggregates for Concrete (UK / Europe)
  • IS 383: Specification for Coarse and Fine Aggregates (India)
  • AS 2758.1: Aggregates and Rock for Engineering Purposes (Australia)
  • ASTM C136: Standard Test Method for Sieve Analysis of Fine and Coarse Aggregates

Always check your local building codes before using gravel in structural applications. Gravel quality and gradation directly affect drainage, compaction and load-bearing capacity — using the wrong grade can cause structural failure or drainage problems.

Tools and Equipment Needed

To work with gravel efficiently, you will need the following tools:

  • Essential: Shovel, wheelbarrow, rake (landscape rake or grading rake), tamper (hand tamper or plate compactor), level, tape measure, garden hose (for settling)
  • Recommended: Landscape fabric (weed barrier under gravel), edging (to contain gravel), plate compactor (for large driveways and patios), vibrating plate
  • Safety: Dust mask (silica dust is harmful), safety glasses, gloves, steel-toe boots, sun protection
  • Optional: Mechanical spreader (for accurate application), laser level, string line and stakes (for depth control)

Safety first: gravel dust contains silica, which is harmful to lungs. Always wear a dust mask when handling dry gravel, especially in enclosed spaces. Use a wetting agent (garden hose) to suppress dust during spreading.

Maintenance and Care

Proper maintenance ensures gravel performs as intended:

  • Rake regularly: Rake gravel back into place, especially on driveways and walkways. This prevents ruts and bare spots.
  • Re-compact annually: Gravel settles over time. Use a plate compactor or hand tamper once a year to firm it up.
  • Add new gravel: Every 2–3 years, top up gravel with a fresh 1/2 to 1-inch layer to maintain appearance and depth.
  • Prevent weeds: Use landscape fabric under gravel to prevent weeds. If weeds appear, pull them by hand or use a targeted herbicide.
  • Control drainage: Gravel drains well, but pooling indicates the base is not properly sloped. Regrade if needed.
  • Use edging: Install edging to prevent gravel from spreading into lawns and garden beds.
  • Snow removal: Do not use a metal snow shovel on gravel. Use a plastic shovel or a snow blower with skid shoes.
  • Avoid heavy vehicles: Unless properly compacted, avoid heavy vehicles on loose gravel driveways.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced contractors make mistakes when calculating gravel quantities. Here are the most common ones:

  • Forgetting compaction: Gravel compacts by 15–20% when tamped. Always add waste allowance for compaction.
  • Using the wrong gravel type: Crushed stone for driveways, pea gravel for walkways, river rock for decorative features. Wrong type = poor results.
  • Ignoring density differences: Different gravel types have different densities. Weight varies by up to 15% between types.
  • Not installing a base layer: Driveways need a 4-inch base layer of larger crushed stone before the top gravel layer.
  • Skipping landscape fabric: Weeds grow through gravel without fabric barrier. Fabric prevents this and reduces maintenance.
  • Applying gravel too thin: Driveways need 4 inches minimum. Walkways need 2–3 inches. Too thin = poor performance.
  • Underestimating waste: Always add 5–15% for spillage and uneven subgrade.
  • Not compacting gravel: Loose gravel shifts and ruts. Compaction is essential for driveways and patios.
  • Rounding down: Always round up — never down — when ordering gravel.
  • Mixing metric and imperial: Keep all measurements in the same unit system throughout. Our calculator handles conversions automatically.
