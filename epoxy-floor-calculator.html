Quick Answer: How Do I Calculate Epoxy Volume?

To calculate epoxy, you need to know that 1 gallon of standard epoxy covers approximately 160 square feet at a thickness of 10 mils. First, measure your floor area (Length × Width). Multiply the area by your desired thickness in mils, then divide by 160. This gives you the exact number of gallons needed. The Science of Epoxy Flooring

Epoxy is not paint. It is a two-part thermosetting polymer made from a resin and a hardener. When mixed together in the correct ratio, a chemical reaction occurs (exothermic curing) that transforms the liquid into a rigid, durable plastic. This is why epoxy provides superior adhesion, chemical resistance, and compressive strength compared to standard concrete sealers.

However, epoxy is highly sensitive to thickness. If applied too thin (under 8 mils), it will peel or wear away quickly. If applied too thick (over 20 mils for a single coat), it can trap air, cure unevenly, and cause bubbling or "fish eyes."

Professional installers typically use a multi-layer system:

  • Primer Coat: 5-10 mils (penetrates the concrete).
  • Base Coat: 10-20 mils (the main protective layer, often mixed with color flakes).
  • Topcoat (Clear): 8-12 mils (provides UV resistance and a glossy finish).

Our calculator allows you to input your exact thickness to ensure you order the correct amount of resin for your specific system.

Global Epoxy Standards: US vs. UK vs. Australia

One of the biggest challenges in sourcing epoxy internationally is that the United States uses the Imperial system (mils), while the UK and Australia use the Metric system (microns or millimeters). If you buy an American kit but use a British tutorial, your math will be completely off.

The Math: Density, Volume, and Coverage

Unlike paint where you just divide by square footage, epoxy calculation involves volumetric math.

A standard 100% solids epoxy has a density of roughly 9.5 to 10 lbs per gallon (or 1.1 to 1.2 kg per liter). Because it is 100% solids, it does not evaporate; what you pour is what cures on the floor.

The Coverage Formula:
1 Gallon = 1604 square feet at 1 mil thickness.
Therefore, 1 Gallon = 160 square feet at 10 mils thickness.

If you have a 500 sq ft garage and want a 16-mil base coat (which is standard for a 1-coat system):
(500 sq ft × 16 mils) ÷ 160 = 50 Gallons. (This is why you must always buy the correct kit sizes. Our tool rounds up to the nearest full kit to ensure you don't run short mid-pour.) If you need to calculate the concrete slab itself first, use our concrete calculator.

Substrate Preparation: The Invisible 80%

Epoxy will only bond to concrete that is clean, dry, and mechanically profiled. If you apply epoxy to a smooth, sealed, or damp floor, it will peel off like a sheet of plastic within a few months.

Professional installers use a process called CSP (Concrete Surface Profile). For epoxy, you need a CSP 3 to 4, which is achieved by diamond grinding or shot blasting. Acid etching is the old-school method, but it is highly unreliable and often voids manufacturer warranties.

Furthermore, you must test for moisture vapor emission. If your concrete slab emits more than 3-5 lbs of moisture per 1000 sq ft per 24 hours, you must use a specialized moisture-mitigating primer. Understanding your concrete slab's integrity is step one; if you are pouring a new slab, calculate your needs with our sonotube calculator or concrete tools first.

The Perfect Mix: Resin and Hardener Ratios

Epoxy is a two-component system. It is supplied as Part A (Resin) and Part B (Hardener). When you buy a "1 Gallon Kit," you are not buying 1 gallon of resin and 1 gallon of hardener; you are buying a total combined volume of 1 gallon.

The mixing ratio varies by manufacturer. The most common ratios are:

  • 2:1 Ratio: 2 parts Resin to 1 part Hardener (by volume). This is standard for most 100% solids epoxy kits.
  • 1:1 Ratio: Equal parts Resin and Hardener. Common for fast-setting or specialized formulations.
  • 3:1 or 4:1 Ratio: Used for some high-performance industrial epoxies or polyaspartic topcoats.

Never guess the ratio. If you add too much hardener, the epoxy will cure too fast, overheat (exotherm), and crack. If you add too little hardener, the floor will remain permanently sticky and never cure. Our calculator uses the total volume required, but you must follow the specific mixing instructions on your kit's label.

Mixing Best Practices

You cannot simply stir the two parts together for 30 seconds. You must mix aggressively for a full 3 to 5 minutes using a drill-mounted mixing paddle (Jiffy mixer) at a low RPM (under 500 RPM) to avoid whipping air into the mixture.
The Two-Bucket Method: Professionals pour the mixed epoxy into a second clean bucket and mix for another minute. This scrapes the unmixed resin from the sides of the first bucket, ensuring a perfect chemical bond.

Application: Spreading and Back-Rolling

Epoxy is heavy and thick. You don't "paint" it on like latex wall paint. You "spread" it.

The standard application technique is:

  • Pour and Spread: Pour ribbons of epoxy across the floor and use a squeegee or a flat trowel to spread it out at the correct thickness.
  • Back-Roll: Immediately after spreading, use a high-quality 3/8" or 1/2" nap roller (or a spiked roller for degassing) to roll back over the epoxy. This ensures an even coat, releases trapped air, and gives the floor a uniform texture for better adhesion. Never skip back-rolling.
  • Spiked Shoes: Once the epoxy is on the floor, you cannot walk on it. Use plastic spiked shoes (golf shoes with spikes) to walk across the wet floor while applying flakes or checking for puddles.

Safety and Pot Life: The Clock is Ticking

Once the resin and hardener are mixed, the chemical reaction begins. This is called the Pot Life—the amount of time you have to apply the epoxy before it becomes too thick to spread.

Pot life is highly dependent on temperature. A standard kit might have a pot life of 30 minutes at 75°F (24°C). However, at 85°F (29°C), that pot life might drop to 15 minutes.

Safety Warning: Mixed epoxy in a bucket generates intense heat (exotherm). Never leave a large batch of mixed epoxy sitting in a bucket; it can melt the plastic bucket and cause burns or release toxic fumes. Always mix small batches and pour it onto the floor immediately.

Wear nitrile gloves (not latex), safety glasses, and a respirator (if applying in a closed space). Epoxy fumes are irritating to the respiratory system. Ensure good ventilation. If you are working on a larger renovation, ensure your drywall and framing are done first; use our drywall calculator if needed.

Costing: DIY Kits vs. Professional Installation

Epoxy flooring is incredibly durable and is often more cost-effective than tile or hardwood. A standard DIY epoxy kit (covering 250 sq ft) typically costs between $100 and $200 in the US, £100-£200 in the UK, or $200-$400 AUD in Australia, depending on the brand and whether it includes flakes.

However, if you hire a professional to diamond-grind, prime, base coat, broadcast flakes, and apply a polyaspartic topcoat, the cost can be 4 to 6 times higher.

Frequently Asked Questions

Epoxy Flooring FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about epoxy volume, mixing, and application.

01

How much epoxy do I need for a 2-car garage?
A standard 2-car garage is roughly 400 to 500 square feet. For a standard 1-coat system at 16 mils thickness, you will need about 3 to 4 gallons of epoxy (including a 10% waste factor). However, if you are doing a full flake system (which requires a thick base coat), you may need 5 to 6 gallons. Use the calculator above to get the exact kit count for your specific garage size.
02

What is the correct mixing ratio for epoxy resin?
The most common ratio for 100% solids epoxy is 2 parts Resin (Part A) to 1 part Hardener (Part B) by volume. However, some fast-setting kits use a 1:1 ratio. Always read the label of your specific kit. Mixing more hardener than required will cause the floor to cure too fast, overheat, and crack.
03

Can I apply epoxy over old concrete?
Yes, but preparation is everything. The concrete must be fully cured (at least 28 days old), clean, and free of oil or sealers. You must mechanically profile the surface using a diamond grinder or shot blaster (achieving a CSP 3-4). Acid etching is highly discouraged as it voids most manufacturer warranties. Check out our concrete calculator if you are pouring a new slab.
04

Why does my epoxy floor have bubbles?
Bubbles are caused by air escaping from the porous concrete (outgassing) or by whipping too much air into the epoxy during mixing. To prevent this, mix at a low RPM (under 500), pour immediately, and use a spiked roller during the back-roll process. Also, avoid applying epoxy in direct, hot sunlight, which accelerates outgassing.
05

How long does epoxy take to cure?
Standard epoxy is walkable in 12 to 24 hours and can handle light traffic in 24 to 48 hours. However, it takes 7 full days for it to reach its maximum chemical and impact resistance. Avoid washing the floor or parking heavy vehicles on it for at least a week. For paint alternatives, check our paint coverage calculator.
06

Do I need a moisture barrier under epoxy?
If your concrete slab is on-grade and lacks a vapor barrier beneath it, moisture can wick up through the concrete and peel the epoxy. You must perform a calcium chloride moisture test. If the emission rate is greater than 3-5 lbs/1000 sq ft/24 hrs, you must apply a specialized moisture-mitigating epoxy primer before your base coat.
07

What is the difference between mils and microns?
A "mil" is an Imperial unit of measurement equal to one-thousandth of an inch (0.001"). It is primarily used in the USA. A "micron" (μm) is a Metric unit equal to one-thousandth of a millimeter. It is used in the UK and Australia. To convert mils to microns, multiply by 25.4 (e.g., 10 mils = 254 microns). If you spot an error in our guide, please report it to our team.
