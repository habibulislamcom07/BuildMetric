What Is a Tile Calculator?

A tile calculator is a tool that estimates how many tiles — and how many boxes of tiles — you need to cover a floor or wall. Instead of guessing and ending up with too few tiles (which means a trip back to the store and the risk of a colour-batch mismatch) or too many (which means wasted money sitting in your garage), you enter your room dimensions and tile size, and the calculator works out the exact quantity.

Tile quantity depends on several factors: the total surface area to be covered, the size of each tile, the grout line width, the layout pattern (straight, diagonal or herringbone), and the wastage allowance for cuts and breakages. This calculator takes all of these into account so you get a reliable estimate before you buy.

Tiles are one of the most popular flooring and wall-covering materials in the world — from ceramic and porcelain to natural stone and glass. Each type has its own installation method, but the maths behind how many tiles you need is the same. This calculator works for ceramic, porcelain, marble, granite, travertine, slate, quartz and glass tiles. It also works for floor tiles, wall tiles, backsplash tiles, shower tiles and outdoor patio tiles.

Whether you are tiling a small bathroom in London, a large open-plan kitchen in Texas, a commercial lobby in Dubai or a garden patio in Sydney, this calculator gives you an accurate estimate in both imperial and metric units.

How to Use This Tile Calculator

Using this tile calculator is straightforward. Follow these five steps:

  1. Enter the room dimensions. Measure the length and width of the floor or wall you are tiling. For an irregularly shaped room, divide it into rectangles, calculate each area separately, and add the results together. Provide the dimensions in your chosen unit system — feet/inches or metres/centimetres.
  2. Enter the tile size. Measure a single tile's length and width. Standard tile sizes include 12×12 in (30×30 cm), 18×18 in (45×45 cm), 24×24 in (60×60 cm) and 12×24 in (30×60 cm). If you are using a mosaic or small format tile, enter the dimensions of the full sheet.
  3. Choose the layout pattern. Straight lay (also called grid or stack bond) needs 10% wastage. Diagonal lay needs 15%. Complex patterns like herringbone, chevron or basketweave need 20%. If you are unsure, choose the next highest category — it is better to have spare tiles than to run short.
  4. Set tiles per box. Check the tile packaging for the number of tiles in one box. This varies widely — small mosaic tiles may come 20 per box, while large format tiles may come 2 or 3 per box. If you cannot find the number, check the product listing online or ask the supplier.
  5. Click Calculate. The calculator returns the total area, tiles needed without wastage, wastage tiles, total tiles to order, and the number of boxes required.

Pro Tip: Always order 1–2 extra boxes beyond the calculated quantity. Tile batches can vary slightly in colour and size, and having spares on hand means you can replace a cracked tile years later without a visible mismatch.

Formula Used by This Calculator

All calculations are performed internally in metric units, then converted back to your preferred unit system for display. The following formulas are used:

Step 1 — Total Area to be Tiled

Total Area = Room Length × Room Width For irregular rooms, divide into rectangles and sum the areas.

Step 2 — Area of a Single Tile

Tile Area = Tile Length × Tile Width Both dimensions must be in the same unit.

Step 3 — Tiles Needed (Without Wastage)

Tiles Needed = Total Area ÷ Tile Area Round up to the nearest whole tile — you cannot buy a fraction of a tile.

Step 4 — Wastage Allowance

Wastage Tiles = Tiles Needed × (Wastage % ÷ 100) Standard wastage: 10% for straight lay, 15% for diagonal, 20% for complex patterns.

Step 5 — Total Tiles to Order

Total Tiles = Tiles Needed + Wastage Tiles Always round up to the nearest whole tile.

Step 6 — Number of Boxes

Boxes Required = Total Tiles ÷ Tiles per Box Round up to the nearest whole box — suppliers sell in full boxes only.

Understanding the Variables

  • Total Area: The surface area you plan to tile, in square feet or square metres.
  • Tile Area: The area of a single tile, calculated from its length and width.
  • Wastage %: The extra percentage added to cover cuts, breakages and future repairs. Higher wastage is needed for complex layouts.
  • Tiles per Box: The number of tiles in one box, as specified by the manufacturer.

Why Round Up, Not Down?

Tile calculations involve rounding in two places: tiles needed and boxes required. Always round up, never down. If your calculation gives 97.2 tiles, order 98. If it gives 4.1 boxes, order 5 boxes. Running short mid-project is far more expensive than having a few spare tiles at the end.

Grout Lines and Their Effect

For tiles with grout lines wider than 3mm (1/8 inch), the grout line itself occupies space and slightly reduces the number of tiles needed. Our calculator uses a standard 2–3mm grout line assumption, which is accurate for most residential installations. For wide grout lines (5mm+), subtract approximately 2% from the total tile count.

Worked Example

Let us work through three examples — a simple straight-lay floor, a diagonal-lay wall, and a complex herringbone layout.

Example 1 — Straight Lay Floor (10 ft × 12 ft, 12×12 in tiles)

  1. Total area: 10 ft × 12 ft = 120 sq ft
  2. Tile area: 1 ft × 1 ft = 1 sq ft (12 in × 12 in tile)
  3. Tiles needed: 120 ÷ 1 = 120 tiles
  4. Wastage (10%): 120 × 0.10 = 12 tiles
  5. Total tiles: 120 + 12 = 132 tiles
  6. Boxes (10 tiles per box): 132 ÷ 10 = 13.2 → 14 boxes

Result: You need 132 tiles, which means 14 boxes (with 8 spare tiles).

Example 2 — Diagonal Lay Wall (3 m × 2.5 m, 30×30 cm tiles)

  1. Total area: 3 m × 2.5 m = 7.5 m²
  2. Tile area: 0.3 m × 0.3 m = 0.09 m²
  3. Tiles needed: 7.5 ÷ 0.09 = 83.33 → 84 tiles
  4. Wastage (15%): 84 × 0.15 = 12.6 → 13 tiles
  5. Total tiles: 84 + 13 = 97 tiles
  6. Boxes (8 tiles per box): 97 ÷ 8 = 12.125 → 13 boxes

Result: You need 97 tiles, which means 13 boxes (with 7 spare tiles).

Example 3 — Herringbone Kitchen Backsplash (8 ft × 2 ft, 3×6 in tiles)

  1. Total area: 8 ft × 2 ft = 16 sq ft
  2. Tile area: 0.25 ft × 0.5 ft = 0.125 sq ft
  3. Tiles needed: 16 ÷ 0.125 = 128 tiles
  4. Wastage (20%): 128 × 0.20 = 25.6 → 26 tiles
  5. Total tiles: 128 + 26 = 154 tiles
  6. Boxes (20 tiles per box): 154 ÷ 20 = 7.7 → 8 boxes

Result: You need 154 tiles, which means 8 boxes (with 6 spare tiles).

Pro Tip: For rooms with many cuts — such as bathrooms with fixtures, kitchens with cabinets, or spaces with diagonal walls — add an extra 5% to your wastage allowance. Cut tiles cannot always be reused, and running out mid-project is far more expensive than having a few spares.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced tilers make mistakes when calculating tile quantities. Here are the most common ones:

  • Forgetting wastage: The single biggest mistake. Always add 10–20% depending on layout pattern.
  • Using the wrong tile size: Measure the actual tile, not the nominal size. A "12-inch" tile may be 11.75 inches in practice.
  • Not accounting for cuts: Tiles at the edges of the room must be cut, and cut tiles cannot be reused if they break.
  • Ignoring colour batches: Tiles from different batches may vary in shade. Order all tiles in one batch, plus spares.
  • Miscalculating irregular rooms: Divide irregular rooms into rectangles and calculate each area separately.
  • Rounding down: Always round up — never down — when ordering tiles.
  • Forgetting grout lines: Wide grout lines slightly reduce tile count, but small ones can be ignored.
