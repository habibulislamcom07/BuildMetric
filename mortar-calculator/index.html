What Is a Mortar Calculator?

A mortar calculator is a tool that estimates how much cement, sand and water you need to mix mortar for a masonry project. Instead of guessing and ending up with too little mortar (which means stopping work, mixing more, and risking a cold joint) or too much (which means wasted cement and sand), you enter your wall dimensions and mortar mix ratio, and the calculator works out the exact quantity.

Mortar quantity depends on several factors: the total wall area, the wall type (brick, block or stone), the width of the mortar joints, the mix ratio (cement-to-sand proportion), the density of cement and sand, and the waste allowance for spillage and uneven joints. This calculator takes all of these into account so you get a reliable estimate before you buy.

Mortar is one of the oldest and most essential building materials in the world. It binds bricks, blocks and stone together into strong, durable walls and structures. From a garden wall to a multi-storey building, mortar is the backbone of masonry construction. Whether you are laying a small patio wall or building a full house, accurate mortar estimation saves time, money and frustration.

Whether you are building a brick wall in Texas, laying blocks in London, constructing a stone wall in Sydney, or tiling a bathroom in Toronto, this calculator gives you an accurate estimate in both imperial and metric units.

How to Use This Mortar Calculator

Using this mortar calculator is straightforward. Follow these seven steps:

  1. Enter the wall dimensions. Measure the length and height of the wall you are building. For an irregularly shaped wall, divide it into rectangles, calculate each area separately, and add the results together. Provide the dimensions in your chosen unit system — feet/inches or metres/millimetres.
  2. Select the wall type. Brick walls, concrete block walls, stone walls and tile installations use different amounts of mortar per square metre. Choose the type that matches your project.
  3. Choose the mortar joint width. Standard joints are 10 mm (3/8 inch). Thin joints are 6 mm and wide joints are 12–15 mm. Wider joints need more mortar.
  4. Set the mortar mix ratio. 1:3 (strong, below grade), 1:4 (standard, most brick walls), 1:5 (general-purpose) or 1:6 (light, interior). The ratio determines how much cement you need per unit of sand.
  5. Set the waste allowance. Use 5% for simple straight walls, 10% for standard walls (recommended), and 15% for walls with many corners, openings or decorative patterns.
  6. Choose your unit system. Use the toggle at the top to switch between Imperial (feet, inches, pounds) and Metric (metres, millimetres, kilograms). Your preference is saved for next time.
  7. Click Calculate. The result shows the total wall area, mortar volume, cement required (kg and bags), sand required (kg and tons), water required (litres), and pre-mixed mortar bags (if applicable).

Pro Tip: Always mix a small test batch first to check consistency. Mortar should be workable but not runny — it should hold its shape on the trowel. Add water gradually; too much water weakens the mortar. If the mix is too stiff, add a little water; if too wet, add a little more dry mix.

Formula Used by This Calculator

All calculations are performed internally in metric units, then converted back to your preferred unit system for display. The following formulas are used:

Step 1 — Wall Area

Wall Area = Wall Length × Wall Height For irregular walls, divide into rectangles and sum the areas.

Step 2 — Mortar Volume per Square Metre

Mortar Factor (per m²): Brick wall (10 mm joint): 0.03 m³
Block wall (200 mm): 0.02 m³
Stone wall: 0.05 m³
Tile installation: 0.005 m³
Adjust proportionally for joint widths other than 10 mm.

Step 3 — Total Mortar Volume

Mortar Volume = Wall Area × Mortar Factor × (Joint Width ÷ 10) Total Mortar = Mortar Volume × (1 + Waste% ÷ 100)

Step 4 — Cement Required

Cement Volume = Total Mortar ÷ (1 + Sand Ratio) Cement Weight = Cement Volume × 1,440 kg/m³ Where Sand Ratio = 3, 4, 5 or 6 (depending on mix).

Step 5 — Sand Required

Sand Volume = Cement Volume × Sand Ratio Sand Weight = Sand Volume × 1,600 kg/m³

Step 6 — Water Required

Water = Cement Weight × 0.5 Water-cement ratio of 0.5 by weight is standard for mortar.
Add water gradually during mixing.

Step 7 — Cement Bags

Bags (50 kg) = Cement Weight ÷ 50 Bags (94 lb) = Cement Weight (lb) ÷ 94 Always round up to the nearest whole bag.

Understanding the Variables

  • Wall Area: Total surface area of the wall being built.
  • Mortar Factor: Volume of mortar needed per square metre for a given wall type and joint width.
  • Mix Ratio: Proportion of cement to sand by volume (e.g., 1:4).
  • Cement Density: 1,440 kg per cubic metre (90 lb per cubic foot).
  • Sand Density: 1,600 kg per cubic metre (100 lb per cubic foot).
  • Water-Cement Ratio: Weight of water divided by weight of cement (typically 0.5).

Why Round Up, Not Down?

Mortar calculations involve rounding in two places: cement bags and sand weight. Always round up, never down. If your calculation gives 13.4 bags of cement, order 14. If it gives 287 kg of sand, order 300 kg. Running short mid-project is far more expensive than having a few spare bags at the end — especially since stopping and remixing mortar breaks the workflow and can cause cold joints.

Mortar Mix Ratios Explained

The mortar mix ratio is the proportion of cement to sand by volume. Common ratios and their applications:

  • 1:3 (Strong): Used for below-grade walls, foundations, retaining walls and load-bearing structures. Highest strength but lowest workability.
  • 1:4 (Standard): Most common for brick and block walls above grade. Good balance of strength and workability.
  • 1:5 (General purpose): Used for non-structural walls, garden walls and general masonry.
  • 1:6 (Light): Used for interior non-load-bearing walls, partition walls and where flexibility is needed.

Always use the mix ratio specified by your engineer or local building code. Using too little cement weakens the mortar; using too much makes it brittle and prone to cracking.

Worked Example

Let us work through three examples — a standard brick wall, a concrete block wall, and a stone garden wall.

Example 1 — Standard Brick Wall (10 m × 3 m, 10 mm joint, 1:4 mix, 10% waste)

  1. Wall area: 10 × 3 = 30 m²
  2. Mortar factor (brick, 10 mm joint): 0.03 m³/m²
  3. Mortar volume: 30 × 0.03 = 0.9 m³
  4. Waste (10%): 0.9 × 1.10 = 0.99 m³
  5. Cement volume: 0.99 ÷ (1 + 4) = 0.198 m³
  6. Cement weight: 0.198 × 1,440 = 285 kg
  7. Cement bags (50 kg): 285 ÷ 50 = 5.7 → 6 bags
  8. Sand volume: 0.198 × 4 = 0.792 m³
  9. Sand weight: 0.792 × 1,600 = 1,267 kg (1.27 tonnes)
  10. Water: 285 × 0.5 = 142.5 litres

Result: You need 6 bags of cement (50 kg each), 1,267 kg of sand (about 1.3 tonnes), and 142 litres of water.

Example 2 — Concrete Block Wall (10 ft × 8 ft, 10 mm joint, 1:4 mix, 10% waste)

  1. Convert to metres: 10 ft = 3.05 m, 8 ft = 2.44 m
  2. Wall area: 3.05 × 2.44 = 7.44 m²
  3. Mortar factor (block, 10 mm joint): 0.02 m³/m²
  4. Mortar volume: 7.44 × 0.02 = 0.149 m³
  5. Waste (10%): 0.149 × 1.10 = 0.164 m³
  6. Cement volume: 0.164 ÷ (1 + 4) = 0.0328 m³
  7. Cement weight: 0.0328 × 1,440 = 47.2 kg
  8. Cement bags (50 kg): 47.2 ÷ 50 = 0.94 → 1 bag
  9. Sand weight: (0.0328 × 4) × 1,600 = 210 kg
  10. Water: 47.2 × 0.5 = 23.6 litres

Result: You need 1 bag of cement (50 kg), 210 kg of sand, and 24 litres of water.

Example 3 — Stone Garden Wall (5 m × 1.5 m, 15 mm joint, 1:5 mix, 15% waste)

  1. Wall area: 5 × 1.5 = 7.5 m²
  2. Mortar factor (stone, 10 mm joint): 0.05 m³/m²
  3. Adjust for 15 mm joint: 0.05 × (15 ÷ 10) = 0.075 m³/m²
  4. Mortar volume: 7.5 × 0.075 = 0.5625 m³
  5. Waste (15%): 0.5625 × 1.15 = 0.647 m³
  6. Cement volume: 0.647 ÷ (1 + 5) = 0.108 m³
  7. Cement weight: 0.108 × 1,440 = 155.4 kg
  8. Cement bags (50 kg): 155.4 ÷ 50 = 3.1 → 4 bags
  9. Sand weight: (0.108 × 5) × 1,600 = 864 kg
  10. Water: 155.4 × 0.5 = 77.7 litres

Result: You need 4 bags of cement (50 kg each), 864 kg of sand, and 78 litres of water.

Pro Tip: When mixing mortar on-site, always add water gradually and mix thoroughly. Dry mix the cement and sand first, then add water slowly until the mortar reaches the right consistency. Mortar should be used within 1–2 hours of mixing — after that, it begins to set and loses workability. Never re-temper mortar by adding more water after it starts to set, as this weakens the bond.

Types of Mortar

Not all mortar is the same. Different types are designed for different applications and conditions:

  • Type M Mortar: Highest compressive strength (2,500 PSI). Used for below-grade work, foundations, retaining walls and heavy-load applications. Mix ratio: 1:3 cement-to-sand.
  • Type S Mortar: High compressive strength (1,800 PSI). Used for structural walls, exterior walls, masonry below grade and where lateral loads are present. Mix ratio: 1:4 or 1:5.
  • Type N Mortar: Medium strength (750 PSI). Standard mortar for above-grade exterior walls. Most common for brick and block walls. Mix ratio: 1:5 or 1:6.
  • Type O Mortar: Lowest strength (350 PSI). Used for interior non-load-bearing walls and where flexibility is needed. Mix ratio: 1:6 or higher.
  • Type K Mortar: Very low strength (75 PSI). Rarely used except for historic restoration work.
  • Lime Mortar: Traditional mortar made from lime, sand and water. Softer and more breathable than cement mortar. Used in historic buildings and restoration work. Requires longer curing time.
  • Cement-Lime Mortar: Blend of cement, lime and sand. More workable than straight cement mortar. Used for brick and block walls. Mix ratio varies (typically 1:1:6 cement:lime:sand).
  • Refractory Mortar: Heat-resistant mortar for fireplaces, pizza ovens, kilns and industrial furnaces. Made from heat-resistant materials.
  • Thin-Set Mortar: Modified cement-based adhesive for tile installation. Applied in thin layers (3–6 mm) with a notched trowel.
  • Pre-Mixed Mortar: Factory-blended dry mortar that only needs water on-site. Consistent quality, convenient, but more expensive than mixing from scratch.

Industry Standards and Codes

Mortar used in construction must comply with several industry standards and specifications:

  • ASTM C270: Standard Specification for Mortar for Unit Masonry (USA) — defines Types M, S, N, O and K
  • ASTM C780: Standard Test Methods for Preconstruction and Construction Evaluation of Mortars
  • ASTM C109: Standard Test Method for Compressive Strength of Hydraulic Cement Mortars
  • ASTM C144: Standard Specification for Aggregate for Masonry Mortar
  • ASTM C150: Standard Specification for Portland Cement
  • BS EN 998-2: Specification for Mortar for Masonry (UK / Europe)
  • BS 5628: Code of Practice for Use of Masonry (UK)
  • IS 2250: Code of Practice for Preparation and Use of Masonry Mortars (India)
  • AS 3700: Masonry Structures (Australia / New Zealand)
  • TMS 402: Building Code Requirements for Masonry Structures

Always check your local building codes before starting any masonry project. Mortar type and mix ratio requirements vary significantly by region, especially for load-bearing walls, seismic zones and freeze-thaw climates. For structural applications, always consult a qualified engineer.

Tools and Equipment Needed

To mix and apply mortar, you will need the following tools:

  • Mixing: Mortar mixing board or wheelbarrow, hoe or mortar mixer, shovel, bucket, measuring containers (for cement and sand), garden hose
  • Application: Brick trowel, mortar hawk, pointing trowel, jointing tool (for finishing joints), spirit level, masonry line, club hammer, cold chisel
  • Safety: Dust mask (cement dust is harmful), safety glasses, rubber gloves (cement is caustic), steel-toe boots, knee pads
  • Optional: Mortar mixer (for large projects), mortar board carrier, hawk, joint raker

Safety first: cement dust is harmful to lungs and skin. Always wear a dust mask when mixing dry cement, and rubber gloves when handling wet mortar. Cement burns are painful and slow to heal — wash any skin contact immediately with clean water. Keep children and pets away from mixing areas.

Maintenance and Care

Proper maintenance ensures mortar performs as intended:

  • Cure properly: Mortar takes 28 days to reach full strength. Keep walls moist during the first 3–7 days in hot weather. Cover with plastic or wet burlap if needed.
  • Protect from freezing: Fresh mortar should not be exposed to freezing temperatures for at least 48 hours after application.
  • Repoint joints: Over time, mortar joints erode. Repoint them every 25–30 years to maintain weather resistance. Match the original mortar composition.
  • Clean efflorescence: White salt deposits (efflorescence) indicate moisture. Brush off with a stiff brush and address the water source.
  • Inspect for cracks: Small cracks in mortar joints are normal. Large cracks may indicate structural issues and should be investigated by a professional.
  • Avoid pressure washing: High-pressure water damages mortar joints. Use low-pressure washing or gentle cleaning.
  • Keep gutters clean: Water overflow damages mortar. Clean gutters regularly.
  • Trim vegetation: Keep vines and plants away from masonry walls. Roots can damage mortar joints.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced masons make mistakes when calculating mortar quantities. Here are the most common ones:

  • Using the wrong mix ratio: 1:3 is for below-grade; 1:5 or 1:6 for general walls. Wrong ratio = weak or brittle mortar.
  • Forgetting the joint width adjustment: Wider joints need proportionally more mortar. Always adjust the mortar factor.
  • Underestimating waste: Always add 10% for spillage, uneven joints and cleanup.
  • Adding too much water: Excess water weakens mortar. Use a water-cement ratio of about 0.5 by weight.
  • Mixing too much at once: Mortar sets in 1–2 hours. Mix only what you can use in that time.
  • Re-tempering mortar: Never add water to mortar that has started to set. This weakens the bond and reduces strength.
  • Ignoring cement type: Different cement types (OPC, PPC, masonry cement) have different densities and strengths.
  • Mixing metric and imperial: Keep all measurements in the same unit system throughout. Our calculator handles conversions automatically.
  • Not curing properly: Mortar needs moisture to cure. In hot weather, keep walls damp for several days.
  • Ignoring weather: Do not apply mortar in freezing temperatures or extreme heat without protective measures.
