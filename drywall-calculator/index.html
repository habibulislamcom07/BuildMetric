What Is a Drywall Calculator?

A drywall calculator is a tool that estimates how many drywall sheets, how much joint compound, how much tape and how many screws you need to complete a drywall installation. Instead of guessing and ending up with too few sheets (which means a trip back to the store and a visible mismatch in thickness or texture) or too many (which means wasted money sitting in your garage), you enter your room dimensions and the calculator works out the exact quantity.

Drywall quantity depends on several factors: the total wall and ceiling surface area, the size of each drywall sheet, the thickness of the drywall, the number of doors and windows (which do not need drywall), the waste allowance for cuts and breakages, and the number of finishing coats you plan to apply. This calculator takes all of these into account so you get a reliable estimate before you buy.

Drywall — also known as sheetrock, gypsum board or plasterboard — is one of the most widely used interior finishing materials in the world. It is used in residential, commercial and industrial buildings for walls, ceilings, partitions and soffits. Whether you are renovating a single room or building an entire house, accurate drywall estimation saves time, money and frustration.

Whether you are installing drywall in a small bedroom in London, a large open-plan living room in Texas, a commercial office space in Dubai or a basement renovation in Toronto, this calculator gives you an accurate estimate in both imperial and metric units.

How to Use This Drywall Calculator

Using this drywall calculator is straightforward. Follow these six steps:

  1. Enter the room dimensions. Measure the length, width and wall height of the room. For irregularly shaped rooms, divide the space into rectangles, calculate each area separately, and add the results together. Provide the dimensions in your chosen unit system — feet/inches or metres/centimetres.
  2. Select doors and windows. Use the dropdowns to indicate how many doors and windows are on the walls you are drywalling. The calculator subtracts their typical area automatically — a standard door is about 20 sq ft (1.8 m²) and a standard window is about 16 sq ft (1.5 m²).
  3. Decide whether to include the ceiling. Drywall is often installed on both walls and ceilings. If you are only drywalling the walls, choose "No — walls only." If you are drywalling walls and ceiling, choose "Yes — include ceiling."
  4. Choose the drywall thickness. Standard interior walls use 1/2 inch (12.7 mm) drywall. Ceilings and moisture-prone areas use 5/8 inch (15.9 mm). Curved walls use 1/4 inch (6.4 mm). Thicker drywall is heavier and more expensive but provides better sound insulation and fire resistance.
  5. Set the waste allowance. Use 5% for simple rectangular rooms, 10% for standard rooms (recommended), 15% for rooms with many corners or angles, and 20% for complex layouts.
  6. Click Calculate. The calculator returns the total drywall area, number of sheets needed, joint compound required, tape required, and screw count.

Pro Tip: Always order 1–2 extra sheets beyond the calculated quantity. Drywall can be damaged during transport or installation, and having spares on hand means you can replace a damaged sheet without a trip back to the store.

Formula Used by This Calculator

All calculations are performed internally in metric units, then converted back to your preferred unit system for display. The following formulas are used:

Step 1 — Wall Area

Wall Area = 2 × (Room Length + Room Width) × Wall Height This gives the total wall surface area of a rectangular room.

Step 2 — Ceiling Area (if included)

Ceiling Area = Room Length × Room Width Only add this if you are drywalling the ceiling as well as the walls.

Step 3 — Subtract Doors and Windows

Net Area = (Wall Area + Ceiling Area) − (Doors × 20) − (Windows × 16) Standard door: 20 sq ft (1.8 m²). Standard window: 16 sq ft (1.5 m²).

Step 4 — Apply Waste Allowance

Total Area = Net Area × (1 + Waste% ÷ 100) Standard waste: 10% for most rooms, up to 20% for complex layouts.

Step 5 — Number of Drywall Sheets

Sheets = Ceil(Total Area ÷ Sheet Area) Standard sheet: 4×8 ft (32 sq ft) or 1.2×2.4 m (2.88 m²).
Always round up to the nearest whole sheet.

Step 6 — Joint Compound

Joint Compound = Total Area ÷ 100 gallons Approximately 1 gallon of joint compound covers 100 sq ft of drywall (3-coat finish).

Step 7 — Drywall Tape

Tape = Total Area ÷ 1000 rolls Approximately 1 roll of joint tape (500 ft) covers 1,000 sq ft of drywall.

Step 8 — Screws

Screws = Sheets × 32 (walls) or × 40 (ceilings) Based on studs at 16-inch (40 cm) spacing. Add 20% for ceilings.

Understanding the Variables

  • Wall Area: Total wall surface area, excluding ceiling.
  • Ceiling Area: Ceiling surface area (only if drywalling the ceiling).
  • Door/Window Area: Standard deductions for openings.
  • Waste %: Extra material for cuts, breakages and future repairs.
  • Sheet Area: The area covered by one drywall sheet (32 sq ft / 2.88 m² standard).

Why Round Up, Not Down?

Drywall calculations involve rounding in one key place: number of sheets. Always round up, never down. If your calculation gives 14.2 sheets, order 15. Running short mid-installation is far more expensive than having a spare sheet at the end.

Worked Example

Let us work through three examples — a simple bedroom, a larger living room with ceiling, and a complex kitchen renovation with many cutouts.

Example 1 — Small Bedroom (10 ft × 12 ft × 8 ft, 1 door, 2 windows, no ceiling)

  1. Wall area: 2 × (10 + 12) × 8 = 352 sq ft
  2. Ceiling area: Not included (walls only)
  3. Subtract doors: 1 × 20 = 20 sq ft
  4. Subtract windows: 2 × 16 = 32 sq ft
  5. Net area: 352 − 20 − 32 = 300 sq ft
  6. Wastage (10%): 300 × 0.10 = 30 sq ft
  7. Total area: 300 + 30 = 330 sq ft
  8. Sheets (4×8 ft = 32 sq ft): 330 ÷ 32 = 10.3 → 11 sheets

Result: You need 11 drywall sheets. You will also need about 3.3 gallons of joint compound, 1 roll of tape, and about 352 screws.

Example 2 — Living Room with Ceiling (4 m × 5 m × 2.7 m, 1 door, 3 windows, 1/2 inch drywall)

  1. Wall area: 2 × (4 + 5) × 2.7 = 48.6 m²
  2. Ceiling area: 4 × 5 = 20 m²
  3. Subtract door: 1 × 1.8 = 1.8 m²
  4. Subtract windows: 3 × 1.5 = 4.5 m²
  5. Net area: 48.6 + 20 − 1.8 − 4.5 = 62.3 m²
  6. Wastage (10%): 62.3 × 0.10 = 6.23 m²
  7. Total area: 62.3 + 6.23 = 68.53 m²
  8. Sheets (1.2×2.4 m = 2.88 m²): 68.53 ÷ 2.88 = 23.8 → 24 sheets

Result: You need 24 drywall sheets. You will also need about 18 litres of joint compound, 2 rolls of tape, and about 940 screws.

Example 3 — Complex Kitchen Renovation (12 ft × 15 ft × 9 ft, 2 doors, 3 windows, complex layout, 5/8 inch drywall)

  1. Wall area: 2 × (12 + 15) × 9 = 486 sq ft
  2. Ceiling area: 12 × 15 = 180 sq ft
  3. Subtract doors: 2 × 20 = 40 sq ft
  4. Subtract windows: 3 × 16 = 48 sq ft
  5. Net area: 486 + 180 − 40 − 48 = 578 sq ft
  6. Wastage (20% complex): 578 × 0.20 = 115.6 sq ft
  7. Total area: 578 + 115.6 = 693.6 sq ft
  8. Sheets (4×8 ft = 32 sq ft): 693.6 ÷ 32 = 21.7 → 22 sheets

Result: You need 22 drywall sheets. You will also need about 5.8 gallons of joint compound, 1 roll of tape, and about 880 screws (ceilings need more).

Pro Tip: For rooms with many corners, angles or cutouts — such as kitchens with cabinets, bathrooms with fixtures, or spaces with plumbing and electrical work — add an extra 5% to your waste allowance. Cut sheets cannot always be reused, and running out mid-installation is far more expensive than having a few spares.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced installers make mistakes when calculating drywall quantities. Here are the most common ones:

  • Forgetting the ceiling: Many people calculate walls only, then realize they also need drywall for the ceiling. Decide upfront.
  • Not subtracting doors and windows: These openings do not need drywall. Subtract them for a more accurate estimate.
  • Forgetting waste allowance: The single biggest mistake. Always add 5–20% depending on layout complexity.
  • Using the wrong sheet size: Standard sheets are 4×8 ft (32 sq ft) or 1.2×2.4 m (2.88 m²). Some suppliers carry 4×10 or 4×12 ft sheets.
  • Underestimating screws: Drywall screws are cheap but essential. Plan on 32–40 per sheet, more for ceilings.
  • Ignoring joint compound: Joint compound is used for taping, filling and finishing. Budget about 1 gallon per 100 sq ft.
  • Miscalculating irregular rooms: Divide irregular rooms into rectangles and calculate each area separately.
  • Rounding down: Always round up — never down — when ordering drywall sheets.
  • Mixing metric and imperial: Keep all measurements in the same unit system throughout. Our calculator handles conversions automatically.
