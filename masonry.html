Technical Guide

The Fundamentals of Masonry

Masonry is one of the oldest and most durable forms of construction. But unlike timber framing or drywall, masonry requires a deep understanding of material quantities and joint dimensions. Small errors in estimating can lead to massive problems on site.

Nominal vs. Actual Dimensions

The biggest mistake beginners make is failing to understand the difference between "nominal" and "actual" dimensions.

When you buy a standard concrete block (CMU) in the US, it is called an 8x8x16 block. These are its nominal dimensions. However, if you measure the block with a tape measure, you will find it is actually 7 5/8" x 7 5/8" x 15 5/8". Why? Because the nominal size includes the standard 3/8-inch mortar joint that will be placed between the blocks.

When calculating how many blocks fit into a wall, you must use the nominal dimensions (including the mortar joint), not the actual physical size of the block. Our calculators do this automatically.

The Importance of the Mortar Joint

Mortar is the glue that holds masonry together, but it is also a structural component. In the UK and Australia, a standard mortar joint is 10mm. In the US, it is typically 3/8 inch.

If the mortar joint is too thick, the compressive strength of the wall decreases significantly. If it is too thin, the bricks or blocks will not bond properly and the wall may be uneven. A consistent mortar joint is the hallmark of a professional mason.

Why You Must Add a Waste Allowance

Just like concrete, masonry requires a waste allowance. Bricks and blocks are heavy, brittle, and prone to breakage during transport and handling. Furthermore, masons have to cut bricks to fit corners, openings, and bond patterns.

  • Breakage: Expect 2% to 5% breakage on standard bricks and blocks.
  • Cutting Losses: Complex walls with many windows or decorative patterns require more cuts, increasing waste.
  • Mortar Waste: A significant amount of mortar is lost from the trowel or falls into the cavity/ground.

As a general rule, add a 5% waste allowance for simple straight walls, and 10% to 15% for walls with complex features or highly textured stone veneer. Our Brick Calculator includes a customizable waste slider.

Global Code Differences: US vs UK vs AU

Masonry is heavily regulated because it carries structural loads and resists wind and seismic forces.

In the United States, masonry design is governed by TMS 402 (Building Code Requirements for Masonry Structures). It dictates specific mortar types (Type N, Type S) for different applications. Type S is used for high-strength structural walls, while Type N is for general above-grade exterior walls.

In the United Kingdom, the standard is BS 5628 and Eurocode 6. The UK is dominated by cavity wall construction, where an inner leaf (load-bearing) and an outer leaf (weatherproofing) are tied together with wall ties. The design must account for wind loading on the outer leaf.

In Australia, the standard is AS 3700. Australian masonry often uses single-skin construction (often with a rendered finish) and must account for extreme wind classifications and potential seismic activity, especially in the western and northern regions.

The Role of Wall Ties

If you are building a cavity wall (common in the UK), wall ties are critical. They connect the two leaves of masonry together and transfer lateral wind loads to the inner structure.

The spacing of wall ties is strictly regulated. Standard practice requires ties at a maximum spacing of 900mm horizontally and 450mm vertically. However, around openings (like windows and doors), ties must be placed closer together to prevent cracking. Use our Wall Tie Calculator to ensure you order the correct quantity.

The Bottom Line

Masonry estimating is about understanding the relationship between the block and the joint. By calculating quantities using nominal dimensions, factoring in a realistic waste allowance for breakage and cuts, and ensuring your design complies with local structural codes, you guarantee a strong and beautiful finish.

Frequently Asked Questions

Masonry FAQs

01 How many bricks do I need per square meter or square foot?

For a standard UK metric brick (215mm x 65mm) with a 10mm mortar joint, you need approximately 60 bricks per square meter for a half-brick wall. For a standard US modular brick (3 5/8" x 2 1/4") with a 3/8" mortar joint, you need about 6.86 bricks per square foot.

02 What is the difference between nominal and actual brick size?

Actual size is the physical dimension of the brick itself. Nominal size is the actual size plus the intended mortar joint. For example, a US modular brick is actually 3 5/8" wide, but its nominal size is 4" because it includes the 3/8" mortar joint. Always use nominal dimensions when calculating brick quantities.

03 What is the standard thickness of a mortar joint?

In the UK and Australia, the standard mortar joint is 10mm. In the United States, the standard joint thickness is 3/8 inch. Mortar joints that are too thick reduce the compressive strength of the wall, while joints that are too thin prevent the bricks from bonding correctly.

04 How much mortar do I need for a brick wall?

As a general rule, a 1:4 or 1:5 cement-to-sand mix by volume is standard for bricklaying. For every 1000 bricks, you will typically need about 0.3 cubic meters (or roughly 10 cubic feet) of damp mortar mix, depending on the joint thickness and brick type.

05 Why do I need wall ties for a brick wall?

Wall ties are used in cavity wall construction to connect the outer leaf of brickwork to the inner structural wall. They transfer lateral wind loads to the inner structure and prevent the outer wall from bowing or collapsing. They are typically spaced at 900mm horizontally and 450mm vertically.

06 How much waste allowance should I add for bricks and blocks?

You should add a 5% waste allowance for simple, straight walls to account for breakage and minor cuts. For complex walls with many windows, doors, or decorative patterns, add 10% to 15% because more bricks will need to be cut to fit.

07 What is the strongest bond pattern for a brick wall?

English Bond (alternating courses of stretchers and headers) is generally considered the strongest structural bond because no vertical joints align. However, Stretcher Bond (all bricks laid lengthways) is the most common for modern cavity walls because it is the most economical.
