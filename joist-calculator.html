Floor and deck joists are the horizontal structural members that support the floor or decking above. If the joists are spaced too far apart, the decking will sag and the floor will feel bouncy. If the joists are undersized for the span, they will fail under load. Calculating the correct number and size of joists is critical for safety and performance.

The most common question we hear is: "How many joists do I need for my deck or floor?" The answer depends on the deck length, the joist spacing, and the lumber size. This guide explains the exact math and the building codes (US, UK, AU) that dictate joist sizes and spans.

1. The Math: Calculating Joists

To determine the number of joists needed, you divide the width of the deck by the joist spacing, then add 1 (for the end joist).

Formula: (Deck Width ÷ Joist Spacing) + 1 = Number of Joists.

Example: A deck that is 12 feet wide, with joists spaced at 16 inches on center (OC):
Convert 12 feet to inches: 12 × 12 = 144 inches.
144 ÷ 16 = 9 spaces.
9 + 1 = 10 joists.

Note: Always add an extra joist for waste and cuts. Also, the rim joist (the outer edge of the deck) is separate from the interior joists, so calculate that separately if needed.

Lumber Size Max Span (16" OC) Max Span (24" OC)
2x6 9 ft 9 in 8 ft 4 in
2x8 12 ft 7 in 10 ft 7 in
2x10 15 ft 8 in 13 ft 3 in
2x12 18 ft 9 in 16 ft 6 in

Important: These spans are based on #2 Southern Yellow Pine, a common US lumber species. If you are using a different species (like Douglas Fir or Spruce-Pine-Fir), the span may be shorter. Always check your local span tables.

2. Joist Spacing: 12", 16", or 24"?

Joist spacing is dictated by the type of decking or flooring you are using and the live load requirements.

  • 12" OC: Used for heavy loads, diagonal decking patterns, or thick composite decking. This is the strongest option.
  • 16" OC: The standard for most residential decks and floors. It provides a solid feel and meets most decking manufacturers' requirements.
  • 24" OC: Used for 2x6 decking or where the joists are perpendicular to the deck boards. Not recommended for standard 5/4" decking, as the boards will sag.

3. Joist Span and Load Capacity

A joist must be strong enough to carry the load of the floor/deck, the weight of the furniture/people, and the weight of snow (in cold climates). The span (the distance between supports, like beams or the ledger board) determines how thick the joist must be.

The Golden Rule: Never exceed the maximum span for your lumber size. The IRC (International Residential Code) dictates specific span limits for each lumber size and species.

Example: A 2x8 joist can span a maximum of 12 feet 7 inches at 16" OC. If your deck is 14 feet wide, you cannot use a 2x8 joist without an intermediate beam. You must either upgrade to a 2x10 (which can span 15 feet 8 inches) or add a mid-span support beam.

4. Bridging and Blocking

For spans greater than 8 feet, you must install blocking or bridging between the joists. This prevents the joists from twisting and distributes the load across multiple joists.

  • Blocking: Solid pieces of wood (usually 2x material) cut to fit between the joists. Placed at mid-span and near the ends.
  • Bridging: Cross-bracing (wood or metal) installed in an X-pattern between the joists.
  • Rule: Space blocking every 8 feet of span, or as required by local codes.

Continue reading below as we dive into regional codes (US vs UK vs AU), cost estimation, and common joist installation mistakes.

5. Regional Joist Standards (US vs UK vs AU)

Joist sizing and spacing are strictly regulated by building codes. While the math is universal, the standard sizes and span tables vary globally.

United States (IRC & NDS)

The US relies on the International Residential Code (IRC) and the National Design Specification (NDS) for Wood Construction. The standard lumber sizes are 2x8, 2x10, and 2x12 (dimensional lumber). The IRC provides span tables based on lumber species (e.g., Douglas Fir, Southern Yellow Pine) and grade (e.g., #2, Stud). For decks, joists are typically 2x8 or 2x10 at 16" on center.

United Kingdom (BS 5268 & Eurocode 5)

The UK follows BS 5268 and Eurocode 5. The UK uses metric timber sizes (e.g., 47x150mm, 47x200mm), often imported as C24 grade (high strength). Joist spacings are typically 400mm or 600mm, matching standard 1200mm plasterboard and decking sizes. In the UK, joists are often supported by steel beams (universal beams) rather than timber beams for longer spans.

Australia (AS 1684)

Australian joist design is governed by AS 1684 (Residential timber-framed construction). Timber is graded using the "F-system" (e.g., F5, F8, F17), and spans are calculated based on the specific species (e.g., Radiata Pine, Ironbark). A standard joist might be 90x45mm (for short spans) or 190x45mm (for longer spans) at 450mm or 600mm spacing. Australian codes are stricter about termite protection and bearer/joist connections.

6. Material Selection

The material you choose for your joists affects their strength, durability, and cost.

  • Pressure-Treated Pine: The standard for exterior deck joists. It is treated to resist rot and insects. Make sure it is rated for "ground contact" if it is close to the soil.
  • Douglas Fir / SYP: Stronger than pine and commonly used for interior floor joists. It has a higher span rating for the same size.
  • Engineered Lumber (LVL or I-Joists): Made from wood strands glued together. They are stronger, lighter, and perfectly straight. They are excellent for long spans but cost more than dimensional lumber.
  • Steel Joists: Used in commercial construction or very long spans. They are strong and termite-proof but require specialized installation.

7. Cost Estimation

Joists are a significant cost in any deck or floor project, but they are essential for structural safety.

Material Costs

  • US (2x8x16 Treated): $15 to $25 per joist. (A 12x16 deck might need 20 joists = $300-$500).
  • US (2x10x16 Treated): $25 to $40 per joist.
  • UK (47x150mm C24): £15 to £25 per 4.8m length.
  • AU (190x45mm Treated): AUD $30 to $50 per 6m length.

Labor Costs

Installing joists is straightforward but time-consuming. If you hire a carpenter:

  • US: $5 to $10 per square foot (installed).
  • UK: £150 to £250 per day for a carpenter.
  • Australia: AUD $40 to $70 per hour.

8. Common Joist Installation Mistakes

  1. Using the Wrong Span: Exceeding the maximum span will cause the joists to sag under load, potentially leading to collapse.
  2. Missing Joist Hangers: Joists should be attached to the ledger or beam using metal joist hangers, not just nails or screws. Hangers provide superior load-bearing capacity.
  3. No Blocking: For long spans, failing to install blocking allows the joists to twist, which can lead to a bouncy floor.
  4. Improper Fasteners: Using standard screws or nails for exterior joists will result in rust and eventual failure. Always use hot-dipped galvanized or stainless steel fasteners.
  5. Not Accounting for Crown: Wood joists are not perfectly straight. Always install them with the "crown" (the curve) facing up. This helps the floor sit flat and prevents sagging.

Joist Questions Answered

Common questions about joist spacing, spans, and installation.

01 How many joists do I need for a 12x16 deck?
For a 12x16 deck with joists spanning the 12-foot width (spaced at 16" on center), you need about 10 joists (12 ft × 12 in = 144 in ÷ 16 = 9 spaces + 1 = 10). If the joists span the 16-foot width instead, you need about 13 joists. Always add 1-2 extra joists for waste. Use our Deck Calculator for a complete material list.
02 What is the maximum span for a 2x8 joist?
A standard 2x8 (e.g., #2 Southern Yellow Pine) can span up to 12 feet 7 inches at 16" OC, or 10 feet 7 inches at 24" OC. If you exceed this span, the joist will sag or fail under load. Different wood species and grades have different spans, so always check the IRC span tables for your lumber.
03 What is the best joist spacing for a composite deck?
Most composite decking manufacturers require joists at 16" on center. Some newer, stiffer composite boards allow 24" OC, but this is not universal. For diagonal decking patterns (45 degrees), you must reduce the spacing to 12" on center to prevent the boards from flexing. Always check the decking manufacturer's installation guide.
04 Do I need blocking between joists?
Yes, if your joists span more than 8 feet. Blocking (solid wood pieces installed between the joists) prevents the joists from twisting and distributes the load. Install blocking at mid-span and at the ends. This is required by the IRC and most local building codes for spans over 8 feet.
05 What size lumber is best for deck joists?
For a standard deck, 2x8 or 2x10 pressure-treated lumber is the most common choice. A 2x8 works for spans up to 12 feet 7 inches at 16" OC. A 2x10 works for spans up to 15 feet 8 inches. For longer spans, use 2x12 or engineered lumber (LVL or I-joists). Always ensure the lumber is rated for exterior ground contact.
06 How do I attach joists to the ledger board?
Use metal joist hangers (like Simpson Strong-Tie) and the appropriate fasteners (joist hanger nails or structural screws). Never rely on toe-nailing alone to attach joists to a ledger board—it is not strong enough for structural loads and will fail inspection. Our Framing Calculator can help estimate the number of joists and plates needed.
07 What is the maximum joist overhang for a deck?
The IRC generally limits joist overhangs to 1/4 of the joist span or 3 feet, whichever is less. For a 12-foot span, the maximum overhang is 3 feet. Exceeding this limit can cause the joist to fail at the cantilever point. Always check your local code, as overhang limits can vary.
