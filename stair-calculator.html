Building a staircase is one of the most challenging and rewarding carpentry projects you can undertake. Unlike building a deck or framing a wall, a staircase has zero margin for error. If the math is off by even a quarter of an inch per step, the bottom step will be a full inch off by the time you reach the top.

The most common question for DIYers and contractors alike is: "How many stairs do I need, and how do I calculate the rise and run?" This guide breaks down the exact mathematical formulas, building codes, and structural requirements used by professional stair builders in the US, UK, and Australia to ensure your staircase is safe, comfortable, and perfectly fitted.

1. The Anatomy of a Staircase

Before calculating materials, you must understand the terminology. Every part of a staircase has a specific name and function:

  • Total Rise: The total vertical height from the finished floor at the bottom to the finished floor at the top. (This is the most important measurement).
  • Total Run: The total horizontal distance the staircase occupies from the first riser to the last riser.
  • Rise (Riser): The vertical height of a single step.
  • Run (Tread Depth): The horizontal depth of a single step (where you place your foot).
  • Stringer: The diagonal structural member (usually a 2x12 board) that supports the treads and risers on the sides. A standard staircase requires at least two stringers.
  • Nosing: The part of the tread that overhangs the riser below it. This adds safety and comfort by increasing the effective tread depth.

2. The "Comfort Rule" (The 17-18" Formula)

A staircase is not just about getting from A to B; it must feel comfortable to walk on. Humans naturally prefer a specific ratio of rise to run. If the rise is too high, you feel like you are climbing a ladder. If the run is too shallow, your heel hangs off the edge.

The industry standard for comfortable stairs is the 17-inch to 18-inch rule.
Formula: 2 × Rise + Run = 17 to 18 inches.
For example: If your rise is 7 inches and your run is 10.5 inches: (2 × 7) + 10.5 = 24.5. Wait, that doesn't work. Let me correct the rule.
The actual "Blondel" formula is: Rise + Run = 17 to 18 inches (or 25-27 inches for the comfort rule). The standard rule of thumb in the US is: Rise + Run = 17 inches.

Rise Height Best Run (Tread) Comfort Level
7.0" 11.0" Very comfortable, ideal for interior stairs.
7.5" 10.0" Standard residential stair.
7.75" 10.0" Maximum allowed by IRC (US) for residential.
8.0" 9.0" Steep. Used in basements or attics.

The Golden Rule: Never build a stair where the individual rise varies by more than 3/8" between steps. This is the #1 cause of stair-related accidents. Our calculator ensures every step is exactly the same height.

3. Building Codes: IRC, BS, and AS

Stairs are heavily regulated due to safety. If you are building a new staircase, it must pass inspection.

United States (IRC Section R311.7)

The International Residential Code (IRC) dictates strict dimensions for residential stairs:

  • Maximum Riser Height: 7.75 inches.
  • Minimum Tread Depth: 10 inches (measured from nosing to nosing).
  • Minimum Stair Width: 36 inches (measured above the handrail).
  • Headroom: Minimum 6 feet 8 inches of clearance from the stair to the ceiling.
  • Handrail: Required on at least one side. Height must be 34" to 38" from the nosing.

United Kingdom (Approved Document K)

UK Building Regulations are slightly different and use metric:

  • Maximum Riser: 220mm (approx 8.6") for domestic stairs.
  • Minimum Tread: 220mm (approx 8.6").
  • Pitch: The maximum pitch for a domestic staircase is 42 degrees.

Australia (NCC Vol 2)

Australian standards (AS 1657) are strict:

  • Riser: 115mm to 190mm (4.5" to 7.5").
  • Tread: 250mm to 355mm (9.8" to 14").
  • Going + Riser: Must fall between 550mm and 700mm.

4. How to Calculate Stair Stringers

The stringer is the backbone of the staircase. It must be strong enough to carry the weight of the stairs and the people on them. In the US, stringers are typically made from 2x12 lumber. A 2x12 is required because you have to cut notches (called "birds-mouths") out of the board, and a 2x10 is often too narrow to fit the rise and run cutouts while maintaining structural integrity.

Stringer Length Math: To find the length of your stringer, use the Pythagorean theorem: A² + B² = C².
Where A is the Total Rise, B is the Total Run, and C is the Stringer Length.

Example: If Total Rise is 108 inches, and Total Run is 150 inches.
108² + 150² = 11,664 + 22,500 = 34,164.
√34,164 = 184.8 inches (or about 15.4 feet).
You will need to buy a stringer board (like a 16-foot 2x12) that is longer than your calculated C value.

Continue reading below as we cover how to calculate treads, the importance of the landing, and how to estimate the total material cost.

5. Calculating Treads and Risers (The Math)

To calculate the exact number of steps, you divide the Total Rise by the ideal Riser height. Let's say your Total Rise (floor to floor) is 108 inches (9 feet).

Step 1: Calculate Risers. Divide Total Rise by 7.5 (a standard comfortable riser).
108 / 7.5 = 14.4.
Since you cannot have 0.4 of a step, you round up to 15 risers.

Step 2: Calculate Exact Rise. Now divide the Total Rise by the number of risers.
108 / 15 = 7.2 inches. This is the exact height of every single step. It is under the IRC maximum of 7.75", so it passes code perfectly.

Step 3: Calculate Treads. The number of treads is always one less than the number of risers. Because the top floor is the final "step," you don't need a tread board there. So, 15 risers = 14 treads.

Step 4: Calculate Total Run. Multiply the number of treads by your desired tread depth. If you want a 10.5" tread:
14 treads × 10.5" = 147 inches (12.25 feet) of total run.

6. The Importance of Landings

Building codes require a landing (a flat platform) under certain conditions to prevent fatigue and accidents:

  • Vertical Height: In the US, a landing is required if the total rise of the stairs exceeds 12 feet (IRC R311.7.6).
  • Doorways: You cannot have a door open directly onto a staircase without a landing. The landing must be at least as wide as the stair and usually a minimum of 36 inches deep.
  • Changes in Direction: If the staircase turns (L-shaped or U-shaped), you need a landing at the corner.

If you are building an exterior deck staircase, you also need to ensure the bottom landing (the ground) is level and stable, usually a concrete pad, to prevent the bottom of the stringer from sinking into the mud.

7. Handrails and Guardrails

Handrails are not optional; they are a life-safety requirement.

  • Height: The top of the handrail must be between 34 and 38 inches above the nose of the treads.
  • Graspability: The handrail must be easy to grab. A 2x4 lumber is not graspable; it must have a circular or oval profile (usually 1.25" to 2" in diameter) or be a specific handrail molding.
  • Continuity: The handrail must run continuously from the bottom to the top of the stairs. It cannot break at a landing unless there is a new handrail starting.
  • Guardrails: If a staircase is open on one side and the drop is more than 30 inches, you need a guardrail (balusters). The balusters must be spaced so that a 4-inch sphere cannot pass through (to protect children).

8. Material Selection and Costing

Staircases can be built from various materials, which drastically changes the cost and complexity.

Materials

  • Pressure-Treated Pine: Standard for exterior deck stairs. It's cheap but prone to shrinking and warping. Use stainless steel screws to prevent rust.
  • Douglas Fir / Hemlock: Common for interior framing and stringers. It is strong and takes stain well.
  • Oak / Maple / Brazilian Cherry: Used for the visible treads in high-end interior staircases. These are expensive and require precise joinery.
  • Steel Stringers: Common in modern architecture or commercial buildings. They require welding and are usually paired with wood or concrete treads.

Cost Estimation

Building a staircase is labor-intensive.

  • Materials Only (US): A basic exterior deck staircase (15 steps) might cost $400 to $800 in lumber, hardware, and concrete for the landing pad.
  • Interior Custom Stairs: Hardwood treads, risers, and custom handrails can cost $2,000 to $5,000+ depending on the wood species and design.
  • Labor: A professional carpenter charges $50 to $100 per hour in the US. A full staircase installation might take 2-3 days of labor, adding $1,000 to $2,500 to your total cost.
  • UK / AU: Costs are similar in proportion, but calculated in pounds or Australian dollars. A standard timber staircase installation in the UK starts around £1,500.

9. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Even experienced carpenters make these mistakes, which can result in a failing inspection or a dangerous staircase.

  1. Measuring Floor to Floor incorrectly: Do not measure to the subfloor. You must measure from the finished floor at the bottom to the finished floor at the top (including the thickness of the hardwood or tile). A 1/2" error here will ruin the entire layout.
  2. Uneven Steps: If your stringer notches are cut even 1/8" off, the steps will feel uneven underfoot, creating a massive tripping hazard.
  3. Forgetting the Nosing: The nosing overhang (usually 1 inch) is required by code to make the stair safer. If you forget to add this to your tread depth calculations, your stairs will fail inspection.
  4. Using weak stringers: Using 2x10s instead of 2x12s for a long staircase can result in the stairs flexing (bouncing) under weight. Always consult a span table or use 2x12s.

Stair Questions Answered

Common questions about calculating rise, run, and building safe stairs.

01 How do I calculate the number of stairs I need?
To calculate the number of risers (steps), divide your Total Rise (floor-to-floor height) by a standard riser height of 7.5 inches. For example, a 108-inch rise divided by 7.5 equals 14.4. Round up to get 15 risers. The number of treads (the flat part) will always be one less than the number of risers (14 treads).
02 What is the maximum stair rise allowed by code?
In the US, the International Residential Code (IRC) sets the maximum riser height at 7.75 inches and the minimum tread depth at 10 inches. In the UK, the maximum rise is 220mm (8.6"). Always check your local building codes, as they can vary slightly by region.
03 What size lumber should I use for stair stringers?
For standard stairs, stringers should be made from 2x12 lumber. The reason is that you must cut notches (birds-mouths) out of the top edge to seat the treads. A 2x10 is often too narrow to cut these notches while leaving enough solid wood at the bottom edge for structural integrity.
04 What is the "comfort rule" for stairs?
The comfort rule (or Blondel formula) states that for a staircase to feel natural, Rise + Run must equal roughly 17 to 18 inches. For example, a 7-inch rise and an 11-inch run (7 + 11 = 18) is very comfortable. A 7.5-inch rise and a 10-inch run (7.5 + 10 = 17.5) is also standard.
05 Do I need a landing on my stairs?
In the US, a landing is required if the total vertical rise of the stairs exceeds 12 feet. You also need a landing at the top of any exterior stairway, and at the bottom of any interior stairway where a door swings toward the stairs.
06 How do I calculate the length of a stair stringer?
Use the Pythagorean theorem (A² + B² = C²). If your Total Rise (A) is 108 inches and your Total Run (B) is 150 inches, then 108² (11,664) + 150² (22,500) = 34,164. The square root of 34,164 is 184.8 inches. You will need to buy stringer boards that are at least 15.4 feet long.
07 What is the minimum headroom required for stairs?
The IRC requires a minimum headroom of 6 feet 8 inches (80 inches) measured vertically from the sloped plane adjoining the tread nosing. This is to prevent people from hitting their heads on the ceiling or the floor structure above.
