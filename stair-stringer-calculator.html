Quick Answer: How to Calculate Stair Stringer Length?

To calculate the length of a stair stringer, you use the Pythagorean theorem: Stringer Length = √(Total Rise² + Total Run²). First, divide your total rise by a standard riser height (around 7 inches or 178mm) to find the number of steps. Multiply that by your tread run (usually 10-11 inches) to find the Total Run. Our calculator above does this instantly, automatically adding the necessary throat depth to ensure your stringer doesn't break under load.

The Structural Engineering of a Stair Stringer

While most DIYers focus on the visible parts of a staircase—the treads and risers—the true structural integrity of the stairs lies entirely in the stringers. A stringer is the inclined structural member that supports the entire load of the stairs, including the weight of the people using them, the materials, and the dead load of the stairs themselves.

When you cut a stringer, you are removing a significant amount of material to create the "steps." The remaining wood at the inside corner of each step is called the throat. If the throat is cut too deep, the stringer loses its structural integrity and can snap under heavy loads.

This is why a professional framing calculator must account for the "throat depth." The standard engineering rule is to leave at least 3.5 inches (90mm) of solid wood at the throat. Our calculator ensures that the diagonal length it provides leaves you with a stringer board wide enough to accommodate both the rise/run cuts and the required structural throat depth. If you are using a 2x12 board (which is actually 11.25 inches wide), a standard 7-inch rise and 11-inch run will leave exactly enough material for a safe throat.

The Golden Rule of Stairs: The 2R + T Rule

Have you ever walked up a staircase and felt like you were going to trip, or that you had to take tiny, awkward steps? That happens when the stair geometry is wrong. To ensure comfortable and safe stairs, master builders use the 2R + T Rule (2 Risers + 1 Tread).

This rule states that the sum of two riser heights plus one tread depth should equal 24 to 25 inches (in the US) or 600mm to 640mm (in the UK/AU).
For example: If your riser is 7 inches and your tread is 11 inches: (2 × 7) + 11 = 25 inches. This is the "sweet spot" for human stride length.

If your total rise forces you to use a higher riser (e.g., 7.5 inches), your tread must be deeper (e.g., 10 inches) to keep the sum at 25 inches. Our calculator automatically checks this ratio in the background to ensure your stair dimensions are comfortable to climb.

Global Stair Codes: US vs. UK vs. Australia

Building codes exist to prevent injury. The maximum riser height and minimum tread depth are strictly regulated, and they vary significantly across the US, UK, and Australia. If you are building a deck or a house, you must adhere to your local jurisdiction's rules.

Open vs. Closed Stringers

When framing stairs, there are two primary ways to attach the treads to the stringer: open stringers and closed stringers.

Closed Stringers: The stringer is cut out (notched) to perfectly fit the treads and risers. The treads sit directly inside these notches, resting on the horizontal cuts. This creates a very strong, traditional staircase where the sides of the stringer show a jagged sawtooth pattern. This is the most common method for residential framing in the US and Australia.

Open Stringers: The stringer is a straight, uncut diagonal board. The treads are attached to the outside of the stringers using brackets or routed into a groove. The treads overhang the sides. This is often used for a more modern, minimalist look, particularly in the UK and for exterior deck stairs. If you choose open stringers, you do not need to calculate the notches, but you must ensure the mounting hardware is rated for the structural load.

How to Layout and Cut a Stair Stringer

Once you have your calculations from the tool above, the next step is marking and cutting the stringer. This is where precision is paramount. Even a 1/8-inch mistake on the first step multiplies into a massive error at the top of the stairs.

1. Setting Up the Framing Square

Professional carpenters use a framing square with stair gauges (small brass knobs) to mark stringers.

  • Set one stair gauge at the exact Riser Height (e.g., 7.5 inches) on the tongue (the narrow arm of the square).
  • Set the other stair gauge at the exact Tread Run (e.g., 10 inches) on the blade (the wide arm).
Clamp them down tightly. Now, you can "walk" the square up the 2x12 board, marking the sawtooth pattern perfectly for every step.

2. The First and Last Cuts

The most common mistake beginners make is not adjusting the top and bottom of the stringer.

  • Bottom Cut: The very first riser cut must be shortened by the thickness of your tread material. If your treads are 1.5 inches thick, you must drop the bottom of the stringer by exactly 1.5 inches so the first step is not too tall.
  • Top Cut: The top of the stringer must be plumb-cut to sit flush against the landing or rim joist. Remember to account for the thickness of any landing material here as well.

3. Cutting the Throats Safely

When cutting the notches, do not overcut into the throat (the inside corner). Use a circular saw to cut down to the line, then finish the cut with a handsaw or jigsaw. Overcutting past the line will weaken the stringer and create a visible notch on the outside face. Always test-fit your stringer on the deck or landing before you cut the others.

Material Selection: Why 2x12 Lumber is the Standard

The standard material for a stair stringer in residential construction is a 2x12 piece of pressure-treated lumber (for exterior decks) or kiln-dried pine/spruce (for interior stairs). But why 2x12?

A nominal 2x12 board actually measures 1.5 inches thick by 11.25 inches wide. When you cut a standard 7-inch riser and a 10-inch run out of an 11.25-inch board, you leave a solid 3.5 inches of wood at the narrowest point (the throat). This ensures the stringer can support the weight without sagging or snapping.

If you are building stairs with a very high rise or need a longer span between landings, standard lumber may not be enough. In those cases, engineers specify LVL (Laminated Veneer Lumber) stringers. LVL is an engineered wood product that is much stronger, stiffer, and more dimensionally stable than traditional sawn lumber. It is more expensive, but for long spans, it is mandatory for safety.

How Many Stringers Do You Need?

A general rule of thumb for stair stringer spacing is 16 inches on center (O.C.) or less.

  • For stairs up to 3 feet wide, you typically need 3 stringers (one on each side, and one in the middle).
  • For stairs up to 4 feet wide, 4 stringers are often required.
If you are using composite decking for the treads, the manufacturer may require stringers spaced at 12 inches O.C. to prevent the treads from sagging. Always check the tread manufacturer's installation guide.

Headroom and Safety Requirements

A structurally perfect staircase can still fail a building inspection if it doesn't have proper headroom.

Headroom is the vertical clearance measured from the nose (front edge) of any tread to the ceiling or floor structure above.

  • US (IRC): Requires a minimum of 6 feet 8 inches (80 inches / 2032mm) of headroom.
  • UK (BS 5395): Requires 2000mm (approx 78.7 inches).
If your stairs turn a corner or go up alongside an opening, ensure that the person walking up doesn't hit their head on the floor joists above. If the headroom is tight, you may need to adjust the position of the stair landing or increase the total run.

Additionally, all stairs with more than 3 or 4 risers require a handrail. The handrail must be mounted at a height of 34 to 38 inches (86-96 cm) above the stair nosings, and the balusters should be spaced so that a 4-inch sphere cannot pass through them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Stair Stringer FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about stair framing and code compliance.

01

How do I calculate the length of a stair stringer?
You calculate the stringer length using the Pythagorean theorem: Stringer Length = √(Total Rise² + Total Run²). Because stringers have notches cut into them, you must use a board wide enough to maintain a solid "throat" (at least 3.5 inches of wood) at the inside corner of the cuts. You can use the calculator above to get the exact diagonal length instantly.
02

What is the standard rise and run for stairs?
In the US, the IRC code allows a maximum riser height of 7.75 inches and a minimum tread depth of 10 inches. In the UK, the maximum rise is typically 220mm (8.66 inches) and the going is 220mm. In Australia, the NCC allows a maximum rise of 190mm and a minimum going of 240mm. A comfortable, standard residential stair usually has a 7-inch rise and an 11-inch run.
03

What is the 2R + T rule for stairs?
The 2R + T rule is a comfort formula used by carpenters: (2 × Riser Height) + (1 × Tread Depth) should equal between 24 and 25 inches (or 600mm-640mm). This matches the average human stride. If your stairs follow this rule, they will feel natural and safe to climb. For example, a 7-inch rise and an 11-inch tread: (2 × 7) + 11 = 25 inches.
04

How many stringers do I need for my stairs?
For standard residential stairs, stringers should be spaced a maximum of 16 inches on center (O.C.). For stairs up to 3 feet wide, 3 stringers (one on each side, one in the middle) are required. For stairs up to 4 feet wide, you will typically need 4 stringers. If you are using composite decking for the treads, check the manufacturer's guidelines, as they often require stringers spaced at 12 inches O.C.
05

Can I use a 2x10 for a stair stringer?
Generally, no. A standard 2x10 board is only 9.25 inches wide. If you cut a standard 7-inch riser and 10-inch run into it, you are left with a very thin throat that will severely weaken the stringer. A 2x12 (11.25 inches wide) is the standard material because it leaves enough solid wood for structural integrity. If you must use a 2x10, you may need to use a different framing method (like an open stringer with brackets). Check out our deck calculator for framing materials.
06

What is the minimum headroom required for stairs?
Headroom is the vertical distance from the edge of the stair tread to the ceiling above. The US IRC requires a minimum of 6 feet 8 inches (80 inches). In the UK, BS 5395 requires a minimum of 2000mm. Always check this before framing, as insufficient headroom is a common reason for failing a building inspection.
07

Why is my stair stringer calculation slightly off?
If your calculated rise doesn't perfectly divide into your total height, you will get a decimal. You must round the number of steps to the nearest whole number, and then divide your total rise by that number of steps to get the exact riser height for every step. Our calculator does this automatically. If your cut stringer doesn't match the math, it is usually because you forgot to subtract the thickness of the tread material from the bottom cut. If you spot a bug, please report it to our team.
