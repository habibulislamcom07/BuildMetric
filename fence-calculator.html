Building a wood fence is one of the most satisfying DIY projects, but it is also a significant financial investment. The biggest challenge for most homeowners is answering the question: "How many fence pickets and posts do I actually need?"

Unlike interior projects where you can easily make a trip to the hardware store, a fence project requires a massive amount of lumber. If you under-order, you might get stuck with a half-finished fence because the store ran out of your specific wood treatment. If you over-order, you are left with hundreds of dollars of warped lumber sitting in your yard. This guide will walk you through the exact mathematical formulas and structural codes used by professional fence contractors in the US, UK, and Australia.

1. The Anatomy of a Wood Fence

To calculate materials accurately, you must break the fence down into its three main components:

  • Posts: The vertical anchors. In the US, these are typically 4x4 treated pine (often ground-contact rated). In the UK, 100mm x 100mm wooden posts are standard. Posts bear the entire wind load of the fence.
  • Rails: The horizontal supports that connect the posts. Most fences use two rails (top and bottom), but taller or privacy fences may use three. Rails are usually 2x4 lumber in the US, or 47x75mm in the UK.
  • Pickets (or Slats): The vertical boards that create the privacy and aesthetic. In the US, standard dog-ear pickets are 5.5 inches wide (1x6). In the UK, fences are often built using pre-assembled panels rather than individual pickets.

2. The Golden Rule: Post Depth and Frost Lines

The most common reason a fence fails is not the wood rotting; it is the posts heaving out of the ground due to frost. To calculate how much concrete you need, you must first determine your post depth.

The 1/3 Rule: As a general rule of thumb, one-third of the post's total length should be buried underground. For a standard 6-foot high fence, you will use an 8-foot post. 2 feet goes in the ground, and 6 feet stays above.

The Frost Line: In colder climates (like the northern US, Canada, and parts of the UK/EU), the hole must be dug below the "frost line"—the depth at which the ground freezes. If your post hole is too shallow, the freezing ground will push the post upward, destroying your fence.

Region Typical Frost Depth Recommended Post Depth
Southern US (e.g., Texas) 0 - 6 inches 24 inches (2 ft)
Northern US (e.g., Minnesota) 36 - 48 inches 48+ inches (4 ft)
United Kingdom ~450mm (18 in) 600mm (2 ft)
Australia (Temperate) No hard frost 600mm (2 ft)

Concrete Math: A standard 10-inch diameter hole, dug 30 inches deep, requires roughly 2 bags of 50lb fast-setting concrete (or 1 bag of 80lb). Our calculator automatically assumes 2 bags per post to give you a safe estimate. Never use dirt to backfill a post; it will rot and shift.

3. Calculating Posts and Rails

Calculating posts is straightforward, but the math changes based on whether you have a property line constraint.

Posts: If your fence is 100 feet long, and you space posts every 8 feet, you divide 100 by 8. This equals 12.5. You round up to 13 "spaces." Because you need a post at the beginning AND the end of the fence line, you add 1. So, you need 14 posts.

Rails: A standard privacy fence has 2 rails per section. If you have 13 spaces, you need 26 rails. However, rails are sold in standard lengths (usually 8ft, 10ft, or 12ft). If your post spacing is 8ft, you can use one 8ft rail per section. If you have a custom spacing (e.g., 7.5ft), you might have to buy 8ft rails and cut 6 inches off each one. Always buy rails in the length that matches your post spacing to minimize waste.

4. How Many Fence Pickets Do I Need?

This is where most people get confused. Pickets are not measured by square footage; they are measured by "linear coverage" including the gap between them.

To find the number of pickets, you need to calculate the "Effective Width" of a single picket + gap.

Formula: (Picket Width + Gap) = Effective Width.
Example: You are using 5.5-inch pickets with a 0.5-inch gap. 5.5 + 0.5 = 6 inches.
Convert 6 inches to feet (6 / 12 = 0.5 feet). This means every picket takes up half a foot of fence line.

If your fence is 100 feet long: 100 / 0.5 = 200 pickets.
You must then add a 5% to 10% waste factor for damaged boards or angled cuts on hills. Our calculator does this automatically by calculating the total linear inches of the fence and dividing it by the effective width of the picket.

5. Choosing the Right Wood for Your Fence

The longevity and cost of your fence are heavily dependent on the material you choose. In North America, pressure-treated pine and cedar are the most common. In the UK and Australia, different species and treatments are standard.

Pressure-Treated Pine (US/UK)

The most budget-friendly option. Pine is treated with chemicals (like copper azole) to resist rot and insects. It is perfect for the posts (which go underground), but the pickets may warp or twist over time as the wood dries. It requires regular staining or painting to extend its lifespan.

Cedar and Redwood (US)

Naturally rot-resistant and insect-repellent. Cedar is lighter than pine, resists warping much better, and takes stain beautifully. It is significantly more expensive than pressure-treated pine, but a cedar fence can last 15-20 years with minimal maintenance.

Composite and Vinyl Fencing

Growing in popularity globally, composite (wood-plastic blend) and vinyl fences offer the ultimate low-maintenance solution. They never need painting and resist rot entirely. However, they have a high upfront cost and can look less "natural." They also expand and contract significantly in extreme heat (like in Australia).

6. Regional Fencing Standards and Regulations

You cannot just build a fence anywhere; local codes dictate the maximum height, the material, and even which side the "good side" faces.

United States (IRC & Local HOA)

The IRC generally limits residential fences to 6 feet (1.8m) in backyards and 4 feet (1.2m) in front yards for visibility. However, Homeowners Associations (HOAs) often have stricter rules regarding materials and colors. In many states, the "good side" (the side without the structural rails) must face the neighbor. Pool fences are heavily regulated under the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code (ISPSC), requiring self-closing gates and specific picket gaps.

United Kingdom (Permitted Development)

In the UK, you usually do not need planning permission for a fence up to 2 meters (6.5 feet) high. However, if your property borders a public highway, the limit drops to 1 meter (3.2 feet). UK fences are often built using pre-assembled panels, which are slotted into concrete or wooden posts. Using concrete posts and gravel boards is a highly recommended UK practice to prevent rot at the base.

Australia (AS 1926 & Local Councils)

Australian fencing rules are strictly governed by local councils. In New South Wales, the standard maximum height is 1.8 meters. A major consideration in Australia is the Dividing Fences Act. If you are building a fence on a property line, you may legally require your neighbor to contribute to half the cost (unless the fence is over the standard height or made of luxury materials). Pool fences must comply with AS 1926, requiring non-climbable zones and gaps no larger than 100mm.

7. Cost Estimation: Materials and Labor

Fencing is one of the most expensive exterior projects per linear foot.

Material Costs

  • Wood Fence (US): $15 to $30 per linear foot (materials only). A 4x4x8 post costs $10-$15. A 6ft dog-ear picket costs $2-$3.
  • Wood Fence (UK): £40 to £80 per panel (usually 6ft wide).
  • Wood Fence (AU): AUD $30 to $60 per linear meter.
  • Concrete: Fast-setting concrete is roughly $6-$10 per 50lb bag.

Labor Costs

Building a fence is physically exhausting work, especially digging post holes. If you hire a professional, expect to pay:

  • US: $25 to $50 per linear foot (installed).
  • UK: £200 to £350 per day for a fencing contractor.
  • Australia: AUD $50 to $90 per linear meter (installed).

Pro Tip: Before digging, always call 811 (in the US) or your local utility locating service to ensure you do not hit underground power, water, or gas lines.

8. Maintenance and Longevity

To protect your investment, you must maintain your fence. Even pressure-treated wood will eventually rot if left in standing water.

  • Sealing: Apply a water-repellent stain or sealant to your wood fence within the first year, and reapply every 2-3 years.
  • Cleaning: Use a pressure washer on a low setting to remove mold and mildew, but avoid getting too close to the wood grain.
  • Inspection: Check the posts annually for signs of rot or termite damage. If the base of the post begins to soften, it may need to be replaced before the fence collapses.

Fence Questions Answered

Common questions about estimating pickets, posts, concrete, and spacing.

01 How many fence pickets do I need for a 100-foot fence?
For a standard 100-foot fence using 5.5-inch pickets with a 0.5-inch gap, each picket takes up 6 inches (0.5 feet) of space. Divide 100 feet by 0.5 feet to get 200 pickets. You must then add a 10% waste factor for cuts and breakage, bringing your total order to 220 pickets.
02 How much concrete do I need per fence post?
For a standard 4x4 post set in a 10-inch diameter hole that is 30 inches deep, you will need approximately 2 bags of 50lb fast-setting concrete (or 1 bag of 80lb). If you are digging deeper due to a frost line, you may need 3 to 4 bags per post. Never use dirt to backfill a post; it will rot.
03 How deep should I dig my fence post holes?
The universal rule is that 1/3 of the post should be underground. For a 6-foot fence using an 8-foot post, 2 feet goes in the ground. However, in cold climates (Northern US, Canada, UK), you must dig below the frost line (often 36 to 48 inches deep) to prevent the ground from heaving and pushing the post up during winter.
04 Do I need to subtract gates from my fence calculation?
Yes. Gates (especially driveways gates) take up significant linear footage where no pickets are needed. Measure the width of your gate and subtract that from your total fence length before calculating pickets. However, you will still need two heavy-duty posts on either side of the gate to support the weight.
05 What is the best wood for a fence?
Pressure-treated pine is the most budget-friendly and is essential for the posts (which go in the ground). However, for the pickets, Cedar is highly recommended. Cedar is naturally rot-resistant, lightweight, resists warping, and takes stain beautifully, lasting 15-20 years longer than pine.
06 How tall can I legally build my fence?
In the US, the typical limit is 6 feet in the backyard and 4 feet in the front yard. In the UK, the permitted development limit is usually 2 meters (6.5 ft), unless it borders a public highway (1 meter). In Australia, the standard limit is 1.8 meters. Always check with your local council or HOA before buying materials.
07 How do I calculate the number of fence rails?
Most standard privacy fences use 2 rails per section (top and bottom), while taller fences might use 3. Multiply the number of sections (spaces between posts) by the number of rails per section. For example, 14 sections with 2 rails each equals 28 rails. Always buy rails in the length that matches your post spacing (e.g., 8ft rails for 8ft post spacing) to minimize waste.
