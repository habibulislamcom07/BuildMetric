Quick Answer: How Do You Calculate Pavers?

To calculate the number of pavers needed, multiply the length by the width of your patio to get the total square footage. Divide that area by the square footage of a single paver. Finally, add a 10% waste factor for cuts and breakage. Remember, pavers are only as strong as their base; you typically need a 4-inch to 6-inch compacted gravel sub-base and a 1-inch layer of coarse bedding sand.

The Engineering Behind Hardscaping

A paver patio or driveway is not just a cosmetic surface; it is a structural system. When designed and installed correctly, interlocking pavers can support the weight of heavy vehicles and withstand decades of freeze-thaw cycles. However, if the sub-base is inadequate, the pavers will shift, sink, and crack within a single season.

The most common mistake DIYers make is underestimating the base. Pavers themselves are incredibly strong (often exceeding 8,000 PSI compressive strength), but the soil beneath them is not. You must excavate deep enough to accommodate the paver thickness, the bedding sand, and the compacted aggregate base.

Furthermore, water management is critical. The base must be sloped away from your house at a minimum gradient of 1 inch per 4 feet (approx 2%) to ensure proper drainage. If water pools beneath the pavers, it will erode the sand and cause the surface to become uneven. Using a geotextile fabric between the sub-grade soil and the gravel base prevents mud from pumping up into the clean gravel, which is essential for long-term stability.

The Math: Calculating Materials Step-by-Step

To ensure you purchase the right amount of materials, you need to calculate three separate components: the pavers, the gravel base, and the sand.

1. Calculating Pavers

First, find your total area in square feet (Length × Width). Then, calculate the square footage of your chosen paver (e.g., a 12x12 paver is 1 sq ft; a 4x8 paver is 0.22 sq ft). Divide the total area by the paver area to get the base quantity. Add 10% to account for cuts around the perimeter and waste.

2. Calculating Base Gravel

Multiply your total area by the depth of the base (usually 4 to 8 inches). This gives you the volume in cubic feet. Since gravel is sold by the cubic yard (27 cubic feet) or by the ton, you will need to convert this volume. Our calculator handles this conversion automatically, providing you with the exact cubic yards of base material needed.

3. Calculating Bedding Sand

The bedding sand layer is typically 1 inch thick. Multiply your total area by 1 inch (or 0.083 feet) to find the cubic footage, then convert to cubic yards. Be careful not to use too much sand; an overly thick sand layer is unstable and causes pavers to rut. For more landscaping math, check out our gravel calculator and mulch calculator.

The 5-Step Paver Installation Process

Installing a paver system is a physical process, but it requires strict adherence to engineering principles. Here is the standard professional workflow:

1. Excavation and Grading

You must excavate the area to a depth equal to the paver thickness + bedding sand (1 inch) + base depth (4-8 inches) + 1 inch for compaction loss. The excavated area must be graded to the final desired slope. If the sub-grade soil is soft or highly organic, you may need to remove it and replace it with structural fill.

2. Base Installation and Compaction

Lay down a non-woven geotextile fabric to separate the soil from the gravel. Add your crushed aggregate (like 3/4" crushed stone or road base) in 2-inch to 3-inch lifts (layers). Compact each lift thoroughly using a plate compactor. Do not dump all the gravel at once; deep layers will not compact properly, leaving voids that cause pavers to sink later.

3. Screeding the Bedding Sand

Lay 1-inch metal pipes (screed rails) across the compacted base. Dump your coarse concrete sand between the rails, and use a straight 2x4 board to screed (level) the sand perfectly. Remove the rails, fill the small trenches they leave, and lightly smooth them over. Never walk on the screeded sand; start laying pavers from the edge and work your way backward onto the installed pavers.

4. Laying Pavers and Cutting

Lay the pavers in your chosen pattern, keeping consistent joint spaces (usually 2mm to 3mm). For the perimeter cuts, use a wet saw with a diamond blade. A circular saw with a masonry blade will leave rough edges and dust, compromising the finished look.

5. Edge Restraints and Joint Sand

This is the most skipped step in DIY projects. Once the pavers are laid, you must install edge restraints (plastic, aluminum, or concrete curbing) along the entire perimeter, pinned into the base. Without these, the pavers at the edge will eventually shift outwards (a phenomenon known as "lateral creep"), causing the entire patio to unravel. Once the restraints are secure, sweep polymeric sand into the joints.

Why Polymeric Sand is Essential

Years ago, builders used regular masonry sand to fill the joints between pavers. While it worked initially, rain and wind quickly washed it away, leaving open joints where weeds grew and ants nested.

Today, professional hardscapers use Polymeric Sand. This is a mixture of fine sand and special additives (polymers). After the sand is swept into the joints, you mist it with water. The polymers activate and harden, binding the sand grains together like flexible cement.

This creates a water-resistant barrier that prevents weeds, resists erosion, and locks the pavers together to distribute loads more evenly. When calculating your material list, remember to buy a few extra bags of polymeric sand; you will need roughly 1 bag (usually 50 lbs in the US or 20kg in the UK/AU) for every 40 to 50 square feet of pavers.

Costing: Buying Base Materials in Bulk

If you are building a patio larger than 100 square feet, buying sand and gravel in bags from a hardware store is extremely expensive and labor-intensive. Instead, order bulk materials from a local quarry or landscape supply yard.

They will deliver the crushed stone and sand by the dump truck load (measured in cubic yards or tons). Our calculator above provides the exact volume in cubic yards (or cubic meters) so you can call the supplier and order precisely what you need. For heavy driveway pavers, you may want to consult our concrete calculator if you are pouring a solid concrete base instead of a flexible aggregate base.

Build a Patio That Lasts Decades

A beautiful paver patio is an investment in your home's outdoor living space. By getting the material calculations right—from the depth of the gravel base to the number of pavers and edge restraints—you ensure the project stands up to the elements without settling or cracking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Paver Installation FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about paver quantities, base prep, and sand.

01

How many pavers do I need per square foot?
It depends entirely on the size of the paver. A standard 12"x12" paver covers exactly 1 square foot, so you need 1 paver per sq ft. A smaller 4"x8" brick paver covers about 0.22 sq ft, meaning you would need roughly 4.5 pavers per square foot. Use our calculator above to instantly get the exact count for your specific paver size and patio dimensions.
02

How much base gravel do I need under pavers?
For standard pedestrian patios, you need a minimum of 4 inches (100mm) of compacted crushed gravel (like 3/4" minus or road base). For driveways or areas supporting heavy vehicles, the base must be increased to 6 to 8 inches (150-200mm). This base must be compacted in 2-inch layers using a plate compactor. Calculate the exact volume with our gravel calculator.
03

How much sand goes under pavers?
You should use exactly 1 inch (25mm) of coarse bedding sand (concrete sand) over the compacted gravel base. Do not use more than 1.5 inches. If the sand layer is too thick, it becomes unstable, and the pavers will rut or shift under foot traffic. Use our mulch & soil calculator for other landscaping volume conversions.
04

What is polymeric sand and do I need it?
Yes, highly recommended. Polymeric sand is a mixture of sand and polymers that hardens when activated with water. It locks the pavers together, prevents weeds from growing in the joints, and stops ants from tunneling underneath. Unlike regular sand, it will not wash away in the rain.
05

Can I lay pavers directly on dirt or concrete?
Never on dirt. Laying pavers on dirt will lead to immediate sinking, shifting, and weed growth. You must excavate and install a compacted gravel base. Laying pavers over existing concrete is possible, but you need to use a thin layer of sand (or a specialized setting bed) and ensure the concrete has proper drainage, otherwise water will pool and freeze, cracking both the concrete and the pavers. For concrete projects, check our concrete calculator.
06

Why do I need edge restraints?
Edge restraints (plastic or aluminum edging pinned into the ground) are mandatory. They hold the entire paver system tightly together. Without them, the pavers on the outer edge will slowly creep outwards over time, creating wide gaps and causing the whole pattern to fall apart.
07

How much waste factor should I add for pavers?
A standard 10% waste factor is recommended for most rectangular patios. This accounts for cuts around the edges, mistakes, and breakage. However, if you are laying a complex pattern (like herringbone) or have a highly irregular shape with many curves, you should increase the waste factor to 15%.
