Technical Guide

The Basics of Concrete Volume

Ordering concrete is one of the few parts of construction where you get exactly one shot. You cannot run back to the hardware store to grab another bag if the ready-mix truck is already sitting in your driveway.

The Basic Formula: Volume = Length × Width × Depth

Concrete is sold by volume. In the United States, it is almost always ordered in cubic yards (yd³). In the UK and Australia, it is ordered in cubic metres (m³).

The math for a simple rectangular slab is straightforward: Multiply the length by the width by the thickness. However, the tricky part is ensuring all your units match before you multiply.

For example, if your slab is 10 feet long, 10 feet wide, and 4 inches thick, you must convert the 4 inches to feet (4 ÷ 12 = 0.333 ft) before calculating.
10 ft × 10 ft × 0.333 ft = 33.3 cubic feet.

Since concrete trucks deliver in cubic yards, you need to divide your cubic feet result by 27 (because a cubic yard is 3ft x 3ft x 3ft, which equals 27 cubic feet).
33.3 cubic feet ÷ 27 = 1.23 cubic yards.

Our calculator does this conversion automatically, so you can enter your dimensions in inches, feet, or metres without worrying about the math.

Why You Must Add a Waste Allowance

The calculated volume is purely theoretical. In reality, you will always need slightly more concrete than the math suggests. This is why every professional estimator adds a waste allowance.

  • Uneven Subgrade: Soil is never perfectly flat. If your excavation is slightly deeper in one corner, the concrete will fill that void.
  • Formwork Deflection: The weight of wet concrete can cause wooden formwork to bow outwards, increasing the volume required.
  • Spillage: Concrete splashes out of the chute, the pump, or the wheelbarrow during transport.

As a general rule, add 5% to 10% for slab pours, and 10% to 15% for trench footings (where the ground is very uneven). Our Concrete Volume Calculator applies a standard 10% waste allowance by default.

Understanding PSI and Mix Ratios

In the United States, the strength of concrete is measured in PSI (Pounds per Square Inch). In the UK and Australia, it is measured in MPa (Megapascals). The strength is determined by the mix ratio of cement, sand, and aggregate.

Strength (PSI) Mix Ratio (Cement : Sand : Gravel) Common Use
2,500 PSI 1 : 2.5 : 5 Non-structural, footings, fence posts.
3,500 PSI 1 : 2 : 4 Standard slabs, patios, driveways.
4,500 PSI 1 : 1.5 : 3 Heavy-duty driveways, structural columns.

Curing: The 28-Day Rule

Concrete does not "dry" to gain strength; it cures through a chemical reaction called hydration. This reaction requires water. If the concrete dries out too quickly (especially in hot weather), the reaction stops, and the concrete will be weak and dusty.

While concrete sets in 24 hours, it takes 28 days to reach 99% of its design strength. For the first 7 days, you must keep the concrete moist. This can be done by covering it with plastic sheeting, spraying it with a curing compound, or simply misting it with water a few times a day.

The Bottom Line

Concrete estimating requires precision. By calculating the exact volume, adding a realistic waste allowance, and knowing the yield of the bags or trucks you plan to use, you ensure your pour goes smoothly without breaking your budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

Concrete FAQs

01 How do I calculate how much concrete I need?

To calculate concrete volume, multiply the length, width, and depth (thickness) of the area. Ensure all measurements are in the same unit (e.g., feet). Once you have the cubic feet, divide by 27 to convert to cubic yards, which is how ready-mix concrete is ordered in the US. Always add a 5% to 10% waste allowance.

02 How much waste allowance should I add to my concrete order?

For a simple slab on flat, well-compacted ground, add a 5% to 7% waste allowance. For trench footings where the ground is uneven and trenches may be over-dug, add 10% to 12%. If you are using a concrete pump, add an additional 5% to account for the concrete left in the pump lines.

03 How many 80 lb bags of concrete are in a cubic yard?

There are approximately 45 bags of 80 lb concrete in one cubic yard. A single 80 lb bag yields about 0.60 cubic feet of concrete. If you are using 60 lb bags, you will need about 60 bags to make a cubic yard.

04 How thick should a concrete slab be?

For standard residential use (like patios or shed bases), a 4-inch thick slab is typical. For driveways or areas that will bear the weight of heavy vehicles, a 5-inch or 6-inch slab is recommended, often reinforced with rebar or wire mesh.

05 How long does concrete take to cure?

Concrete typically sets within 24 to 48 hours and you can usually walk on it after that. However, it takes about 28 days for concrete to reach its full design strength. It is important to keep it moist during the first week to prevent cracking.

06 Can I mix concrete by hand for large projects?

Mixing by hand or in a small mixer is only practical for very small projects (under 1 cubic yard). For larger pours like driveways or foundations, the labor required to mix that much concrete is immense, and you risk cold joints. It is much more efficient and cost-effective to order ready-mix concrete delivered by a truck.

07 What is the difference between concrete and cement?

Cement is actually just one ingredient in concrete. Cement is a fine powder (usually Portland cement) that acts as the glue. Concrete is the final mixture of cement, water, sand, and gravel (aggregate). You cannot pour just cement; it must be mixed with aggregates to form concrete.
