Quick Answer: How to Calculate Wallpaper Rolls?

To determine how many rolls of wallpaper you need, first calculate the total square footage of your walls (Perimeter × Height) and subtract the area of doors and windows. Divide this number by the square footage of a single roll. Finally, add a 15% to 20% overage if your wallpaper has a pattern repeat. However, the most accurate method is calculating by "drops" (vertical strips) rather than pure square footage, which our calculator above does automatically.

The Mathematics of Wallpaper: Why Square Footage Fails

If you ask a beginner how to calculate wallpaper, they will usually tell you to measure the wall area and divide it by the area of a roll. While this gives a rough estimate, it often leads to buying too little wallpaper, resulting in a frantic search for a matching dye lot when you run out.

The reason is simple: Wallpaper is hung in vertical strips, not as a liquid paint. You cannot use every square inch of a wallpaper roll because you have to cut straight lines, trim at the ceiling and baseboards, and—most importantly—match the pattern.

Professional installers use the "Drop Method." They calculate the total height of the wall, add the pattern repeat, and divide the total length of the roll by this number to see how many usable strips (drops) they can get per roll. If a roll yields 3 drops, and your room needs 10 drops, you need 4 rolls, even if the total square footage suggests 3 rolls would be enough. Our calculator uses this exact drop logic to ensure you never come up short.

Understanding Pattern Repeats: Straight Match vs. Drop Match

The "Pattern Repeat" is the distance before the design repeats itself on the roll. The larger the repeat, the more waste you will have. When selecting your wallpaper, you will see two main types of pattern matching:

1. Straight Match (Horizontal Alignment)

The pattern matches perfectly across the seam at the same height. If the repeat is 12 inches, you simply align the design horizontally. This is common for simple stripes or small geometric patterns and results in less waste.

2. Drop Match (Offset Alignment)

This is where the math gets tricky. A drop match means the design on the next strip drops down by half the repeat to align with the previous strip. This is very common with large floral or damask patterns. If you have a 24-inch drop match, you must cut the second strip so it starts 12 inches lower than the first. This creates massive waste at the top and bottom of the roll.

Global Roll Sizes: US vs. UK vs. Australia

A common mistake when buying wallpaper online from overseas is assuming all rolls are the same size. The United States uses the Imperial system, while the UK, Europe, and Australia use standard metric rolls. Here is how they differ:

USA (Double Roll) 20.5 inches wide × 33 feet long 56 square feet Sold as a "double roll" but is essentially two single rolls joined.
USA (Single Roll) 20.5 inches wide × 16.5 feet long 28 square feet Be careful: Many US brands list the price per single roll, but only sell double rolls.

How to Measure a Room for Wallpaper

Accuracy starts with the tape measure. Follow these steps for a flawless estimate:

  • Measure the Perimeter: Measure the total width of all walls you plan to cover (Length + Width + Length + Width).
  • Measure the Height: Measure from the baseboard to the ceiling. Add an extra 2 to 4 inches to allow for trimming and pattern matching at the top and bottom.
  • Subtract Openings: Measure the area of standard doors (usually 21 sq ft or 2 sq m) and windows. Do not subtract small outlets or light switches.
  • Check for Obstructions: Large built-in cabinets, fireplaces, or archways should be subtracted. However, leave extra paper for complex corners or sloped ceilings.

The Secret to a Flawless Finish: Wall Preparation

Even the most expensive designer wallpaper will look cheap if the walls underneath are not properly prepared. Wallpaper is unforgiving; it highlights every bump, crack, and imperfection. Before you even think about cutting your first strip, you must prep the surface.

1. Remove Old Wallpaper

Never hang new wallpaper over old wallpaper. The moisture from the new paste will cause the old layers to bubble and peel, bringing the new paper down with it. Use a scoring tool and a steamer or chemical stripper to remove all old paper completely.

2. Wash and Repair the Walls

Wash the walls with a mild detergent to remove grease and dust. Fill any holes or cracks with lightweight spackle or joint compound, then sand them smooth. If the walls are highly glossy (like semi-gloss paint), lightly sand them with 120-grit sandpaper to give the paste something to grip onto. For major repairs, check out our drywall calculator to estimate new sheets.

3. Apply Wallpaper Primer (Sizing)

This is the most critical step. Apply a specialized wallpaper primer (often called "sizing"). This does two things: it seals the porous drywall so it doesn't absorb all the paste, and it creates a slip layer that allows the wallpaper to slide easily into place for pattern matching. It also makes future removal much easier.

Choosing the Right Paste

There are three main types of wallpaper paste, and choosing the right one depends on the weight of your wallpaper and the local climate:

  • Pre-Pasted Wallpaper: Common in the US. You simply fill a water tray and pull the roll through it. This is great for light to medium-weight papers, but the built-in adhesive is often weaker than dedicated pastes.
  • Ready-Mixed Paste: Sold in tubs. It's a sticky, gel-like adhesive ideal for heavy vinyl or textured papers.
  • Powdered Paste (Flake Paste): Common in the UK and Europe. You mix it with water to activate it. It's highly economical, allows you to control the thickness (heavier paste for heavier paper), and is generally stronger than pre-pasted adhesives.

Always ensure you are using a paste that is compatible with your wallpaper. For example, natural grasscloth requires a specialized clear adhesive, while standard vinyl can use a standard heavy-duty paste.

Common Mistakes to Avoid (And How We Solve Them)

1. Booking the Paper (Not Numbering Your Drops): When you cut your vertical strips, you MUST write the number of the strip on the back (e.g., 1, 2, 3, 4) before you hang them. If you drop a strip or lose your place, hanging the wrong sequence will break the pattern instantly.

2. Not Waiting for the Paste to "Relax": After applying paste to the back of the wallpaper (if not pre-pasted), you must "book" it (fold it paste-to-paste) and let it sit for 5-10 minutes. This allows the paper to absorb the moisture, expand, and relax. If you hang it immediately, it will expand on the wall and cause bubbles.

3. Using the Wrong Tools: Do not use a hard plastic smoother on delicate wallpaper; use a soft felt smoother. Use a sharp utility knife with a fresh blade for trimming (change the blade often, as paste dulls blades quickly).

4. Overworking the Seams: When smoothing the edges, do not press too hard. This forces the paste out from under the seam and can cause the edges to curl or peel later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Wallpaper Calculation FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about rolls, pattern matching, and waste.

01

How many rolls of wallpaper for a 10x10 room?
For a standard 10x10 foot room with 8-foot ceilings, you will typically need 6 to 8 standard double rolls (or 12 to 16 single rolls in the US). This assumes one door and two windows. If your wallpaper has a large pattern repeat (over 18 inches), you may need to buy 1 or 2 extra rolls. Use the calculator above for your exact dimensions.
02

What is a pattern repeat and how does it affect rolls?
The pattern repeat is the distance before the design repeats itself on the roll. A larger repeat means you have to cut off more waste at the top and bottom to align the design across strips. For example, a 24-inch repeat will create significantly more waste than a 12-inch repeat, often requiring you to buy an extra roll or two.
03

What is the difference between a single and double roll?
In the US, a single roll is roughly 20.5 inches wide by 16.5 feet long (about 28 sq ft). A double roll is 20.5 inches wide by 33 feet long (about 56 sq ft). Most wallpaper is priced and sold by the double roll. In the UK and Australia, wallpaper is typically sold as a single roll measuring 0.53m wide by 10m long.
04

Do I subtract doors and windows from the total area?
Yes, you should subtract the square footage of standard doors and windows. A standard door is about 21 sq ft (2 sq m) and a standard window is about 15 sq ft (1.5 sq m). Our tool automatically subtracts these if you input the number of doors and windows in the room. However, do not subtract small outlets or light switches.
05

How much extra wallpaper should I buy for waste?
It is highly recommended to buy 10% to 15% extra wallpaper. This covers mistakes during cutting, pattern matching waste, and future repairs. It is crucial to buy all your rolls at the same time from the same batch (dye lot) to ensure the colors match perfectly. If you are painting instead of wallpapering, check out our paint coverage calculator.
06

How do I calculate wallpaper for a sloped ceiling?
For sloped ceilings or stairwells, do not use the standard room height. Measure the highest point of the wall and use that height for your calculation. You will have to trim away the excess wallpaper at the bottom or slope side, which creates additional waste. It's best to add an extra 20% waste factor for these complex areas.
07

Can I return unused rolls of wallpaper?
Return policies vary by retailer. Many specialist wallpaper stores allow returns of unopened, uncut rolls within 30 days, but some custom or print-to-order papers are non-returnable. Always check the return policy before ordering. Keep at least one extra roll for future repairs, as dye lots change and you may not find the exact match later.
