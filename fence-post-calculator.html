A fence is only as strong as its posts. Whether you are building a small garden fence or a 6-foot privacy fence, the posts must be anchored deep enough and set in enough concrete to withstand wind, frost, and the weight of the panels. If you under-concrete your posts, the fence will lean, sag, or completely blow over within a few years.

The most common question we hear is: "How much concrete do I need per fence post?" The answer depends on three factors: the size of the post, the diameter of the hole, and how deep you dig. This guide walks through the exact math, frost line requirements (US, UK, AU), and the concrete mixes used by professional fence contractors.

1. The Golden Rule of Fence Post Holes

Before calculating concrete, you must determine the hole size. The two golden rules of fence post installation are:

  • The 1/3 Rule: One-third of the post's total length should be underground. For a 6-foot fence using an 8-foot post, 2 feet goes in the ground.
  • The 3x Rule: The hole diameter should be 3 times the width of the post. For a 4x4 post (which is actually 3.5" wide), the hole should be about 10 to 12 inches in diameter. This provides a stable "bell" of concrete that resists frost heave and wind uplift.
Post Size Min Hole Dia. Concrete (30" deep)
4x4 (3.5") 10 inches 2 bags (50lb)
4x4 (3.5") 12 inches 3 bags (50lb)
6x6 (5.5") 12 inches 3 bags (50lb)
6x6 (5.5") 14 inches 4 bags (50lb)

The Formula: Volume of cylinder = π × r² × h. For a 10-inch hole that is 30 inches deep: r = 5, h = 30. Volume = 3.14 × 25 × 30 = 2,355 cubic inches. Divide by 1,728 (cubic inches in a cubic foot) = 1.36 cubic feet. A 50lb bag of fast-setting concrete yields about 0.37 cubic feet, so 1.36 / 0.37 = 3.6 bags. (We round up to 4 for waste).

2. The Frost Line: The Most Critical Factor

If you live in a cold climate, you cannot just dig a hole 2 feet deep and call it done. The ground freezes in winter, expands, and pushes the post upward. This is called "frost heave." To prevent it, your post hole must be dug below the frost line.

US Frost Lines

  • Southern States (FL, TX, AZ): 6-12 inches.
  • Mid-Atlantic (VA, MD, PA): 24-36 inches.
  • Northern States (NY, MN, ME): 48+ inches.
  • Mountain West (CO, UT): 36-48 inches.

UK and Australian Frost Lines

The UK rarely experiences deep frost, but the standard practice is to dig fence post holes 600mm (24 inches) deep. In Australia, frost is only an issue in the Australian Alps and Tasmania. For most of AU, a depth of 600mm to 900mm is used based on wind load requirements.

3. Concrete Types: Fast-Setting vs. Standard

You should never use standard mortar mix or pure cement to set fence posts. You need either fast-setting concrete or a standard concrete mix.

Fast-Setting Concrete (e.g., Quikrete Fast-Setting)

The most popular choice for DIY fence posts. You pour the dry mix into the hole, add water, and it hardens in 20-40 minutes. No mixing required. Each 50lb bag yields about 0.37 cubic feet of concrete. This is the standard for residential fences.

Standard Concrete Mix (e.g., Quikrete Concrete Mix)

Requires you to mix the concrete in a wheelbarrow with water before pouring. It sets in 24 hours and has a higher compressive strength. Best for commercial fences or very heavy gates.

Gravel and Packed Soil

Some contractors use compacted gravel instead of concrete. This allows water to drain away from the post, reducing rot. However, gravel provides less lateral support against wind. It is often used in the UK for wooden posts and is increasingly popular in the US for privacy fences in wet climates.

4. Setting the Posts: Best Practices

  1. Call 811 (Before You Dig): In the US, call 811 to have underground utilities marked. Hitting a gas or power line while digging is deadly.
  2. Dig Deeper Than You Need: Add 6 inches to your calculated depth to account for a gravel base (for drainage).
  3. Add Gravel First: Put 6 inches of gravel at the bottom of the hole. This drains water away from the bottom of the post.
  4. Set the Post with Wood Blocks: Use scrap wood to hold the post plumb (straight) and level while the concrete hardens.
  5. Crown the Concrete: Slope the concrete slightly away from the post so water runs off, not toward it.
  6. Leave a Gap: The bottom of the post should sit on the gravel, not directly on the concrete. Concrete is porous; if the post sits directly in concrete, the water in the concrete will rot the wood.

5. Choosing the Right Wood for Fence Posts

The concrete you pour is only half the equation. The post itself must be capable of surviving decades of ground contact.

Pressure-Treated Pine (US/UK)

The standard choice. Look for posts rated "Ground Contact" or UC4B (in the US). This means the wood is chemically treated to resist rot and termites when buried. Standard treated wood (above-ground rating) will rot within 3-5 years if placed in the ground. Never use untreated pine for fence posts.

Cedar and Redwood (US)

Naturally rot-resistant. Cedar posts can last 15-20 years in the ground without chemical treatment. However, they are more expensive and softer than treated pine. They are best used for decorative fences where the wood grain will be visible.

Concrete Posts (UK/AU)

In the UK and Australia, it is very common to use concrete posts (often called concrete spurs or concrete fence posts). These are slotted to hold wooden fence panels. They never rot, are extremely strong, and require less concrete to set because they are heavier. UK concrete posts are often set in a mix of gravel and concrete for drainage.

Metal Posts (Global)

Steel or aluminum posts are increasingly popular in the US for modern privacy fences. They are set in concrete just like wood posts but never rot. They require a specific bracket to attach wood fence panels. They cost more upfront but last 50+ years.

6. Regional Fence Post Standards

United States (IRC)

The International Residential Code (IRC) dictates fence height and post depth. Most municipalities limit residential fence height to 6 feet in the backyard and 4 feet in the front yard. For posts, the IRC requires that the post be embedded at least 1/3 of its total length below grade. For a 6-foot fence (8-foot post), that means a 2-foot hole minimum. In frost zones, the hole must extend below the frost line.

United Kingdom (Building Regulations)

UK fences generally do not need planning permission if they are under 2 meters (6.5 feet) high. Posts are typically set at 600mm (2 feet) deep in a 300mm (12 inch) diameter hole. UK contractors often use a mix of gravel and concrete (called "postcrete" in some regions) to allow drainage, which is critical in the UK's wet climate.

Australia (AS 1926)

Australian fences must comply with local council rules, often limited to 1.8 meters (5.9 feet). Fence posts are set in concrete at 600-900mm depth, depending on the wind zone. Coastal areas require stainless steel or hot-dipped galvanized fasteners to prevent rust. Pool fences are strictly regulated under AS 1926 (no climbable surfaces, self-closing gates).

7. Cost Estimation

Fence posts and concrete are a small portion of the total fence cost, but getting them wrong is the most expensive mistake.

Material Costs

  • 4x4x8 Treated Post (US): $10 to $20 per post.
  • 6x6x8 Treated Post (US): $25 to $45 per post.
  • 50lb Fast-Setting Concrete (US): $6 to $10 per bag. (You need 2-3 bags per post).
  • UK Concrete Post: £15 to £30 per post.
  • AU Treated Post: AUD $20 to $40 per post.

Labor Costs

Setting posts is hard, backbreaking work. If you hire a contractor to dig and set posts, expect to pay:

  • US: $25 to $50 per post (installed).
  • UK: £30 to £60 per post (installed).
  • Australia: AUD $40 to $80 per post (installed).

8. Common Fence Post Mistakes

  1. Not Digging Deep Enough: A shallow hole will lean or fall over after the first heavy rain or windstorm.
  2. Using the Wrong Concrete: Using standard mortar mix or pure cement will crumble. Use a proper concrete mix.
  3. Over-Concreting the Wood: If the concrete is above the soil line and the post has no barrier, water will sit against the wood and rot it.
  4. Forgetting the Gravel Base: Without a drainage layer, water pools at the bottom of the hole and rots the post from the inside out.
  5. Not Calling 811 (US): Hitting a gas line while digging a fence post hole is a catastrophic and dangerous mistake.

Fence Post Questions Answered

Common questions about post depth, concrete quantity, and frost lines.

01 How much concrete do I need per fence post?
For a standard 4x4 post set in a 10-inch diameter hole that is 30 inches deep, you will need approximately 2 to 3 bags of 50lb fast-setting concrete (or 1-2 bags of 80lb). If your hole is deeper due to a frost line, or if you are using a 6x6 post, you may need 3 to 5 bags per post.
02 How deep should I dig fence post holes?
The universal rule is that 1/3 of the post should be underground. For a 6-foot fence (8-foot post), 2 feet goes in the ground. However, in cold climates (Northern US, Canada), you must dig below the frost line (often 36 to 48 inches) to prevent the ground from freezing and pushing the post up during winter. In the UK and AU, a minimum depth of 600mm (2 feet) is standard.
03 Should I put gravel at the bottom of the post hole?
Yes, absolutely. Put 4 to 6 inches of gravel at the bottom of the hole. This serves two purposes: it provides drainage so water does not pool around the bottom of the post (causing rot), and it prevents the post from sitting directly on concrete. If you pour concrete directly under the post, water will travel down the post and sit in the concrete, rotting the wood from the inside out.
04 What is the best concrete for setting fence posts?
Fast-setting concrete (like Quikrete Fast-Setting or Postcrete in the UK) is the best choice for DIY fence posts. You pour the dry mix into the hole, add water, and it hardens in 20-40 minutes. It is strong, requires no mixing, and eliminates the risk of a watery mix. Avoid using pure cement or mortar mix, as these will crack and crumble when exposed to soil moisture.
05 How long does concrete take to set for a fence post?
Fast-setting concrete sets hard in 20 to 40 minutes. Standard mixed concrete sets in 24 hours. However, you should wait at least 24-48 hours before attaching heavy fence panels to the posts to allow the concrete to fully cure. Avoid disturbing the posts during this time.
06 How do I prevent my fence posts from rotting?
Use ground-contact rated pressure-treated wood (UC4B in the US). Add a gravel base for drainage. Slope the concrete away from the post (crown it). Some contractors also wrap the bottom of the post in a post protector sleeve. Never use untreated wood for fence posts, and never let soil or concrete sit above the top of the concrete line.
07 Can I use gravel instead of concrete for fence posts?
Yes, but with caveats. Compacted gravel (like 3/4" crushed stone) provides excellent drainage and is used widely in the UK and wet climates to prevent rot. However, gravel offers less lateral support against strong winds and heavier fences. If you use gravel, you must compact it in 6-inch layers with a tamper, and it is best for low fences or decorative fences. For a 6-foot privacy fence, concrete is stronger.
