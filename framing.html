Technical Guide

The Fundamentals of Timber Framing

Timber framing is the skeleton of a building. It is the structural system that transfers the weight of the roof, floors, and walls down to the foundation. Get the framing wrong, and the whole building is compromised.

Platform vs. Balloon Framing

In North America, the dominant method is Platform Framing. The walls of each floor are built flat on the subfloor, tilted up into place, and then the next floor's joists are installed on top. This is fast, safe, and allows for easy prefabrication.

Balloon Framing is an older method where the exterior wall studs run continuously from the foundation to the roof eaves, with floor joists nailed to the side of the studs. While largely replaced by platform framing in the US, it is still common in older properties and is occasionally used for specific architectural features.

In the UK and Australia, timber frame construction is often prefabricated off-site into panels. These panels (open or closed) are delivered to site and craned into position, which speeds up construction and improves quality control.

The Importance of Standard Spacing

Standard spacing (usually 16" o.c. or 24" o.c. in the US, and 400mm or 600mm in the UK/AU) is not just for structural strength. It exists to support the sheathing and drywall panels.

If your studs are set at 16 inches on center, an 8-foot sheet of plywood will land perfectly on the center of a stud at 0, 4, and 8 feet. If your spacing is off by even half an inch, the sheet edges will overhang the studs, requiring wasteful cutting and extra blocking.

Advanced Framing (OVE) and Thermal Bridging

In traditional framing, wood studs take up about 25% of the wall area, and the other 75% is insulation. Because wood is a poor insulator compared to fiberglass or mineral wool, every stud represents a "thermal bridge" where heat escapes.

Optimum Value Engineering (OVE), also known as Advanced Framing, is a technique that reduces the amount of wood used while improving energy efficiency. It uses:

  • 24" o.c. spacing instead of 16" to allow for more insulation.
  • Single top plates with metal straps instead of double top plates.
  • Ladder blocking at wall intersections instead of full studs.

While OVE is common in progressive US jurisdictions, it must be explicitly allowed by the local building code and the structural engineer. In the UK and Australia, prefabricated panels often achieve similar efficiencies through precise off-site engineering.

Global Code Differences in Framing

Timber framing is heavily regulated because it resists wind, snow, and seismic loads. The rules change drastically across borders.

In the United States, the IRC 2021 (Table R602.3(5)) is the primary reference. It dictates allowable spans for floor joists, ceiling joists, and rafters based on species (e.g., SPF, Douglas Fir), grade, and size. It also provides strict nailing schedules for connections.

In the United Kingdom, the standard is BS 5268 (soon to be fully replaced by Eurocode 5). Timber is graded as C16 or C24 (indicating the bending strength in N/mm²). UK codes are highly focused on ensuring the timber remains dry, requiring specific breathable membranes and ventilation paths in roof and wall structures.

In Australia, AS 1684 governs residential timber framing. Australian timber is graded differently (e.g., MGP10, F5, F7) and the code places immense emphasis on tie-downs and bracing to resist high winds and cyclones, especially in the northern regions.

The Load Path: King Studs and Jack Studs

When you cut a hole in a wall for a window or door, you interrupt the load path. To fix this, framers install a horizontal header above the opening to carry the weight.

The header rests on Jack Studs (trimmer studs) which physically support it. Next to the jack stud is the King Stud, a full-height stud that runs from the bottom plate to the top plate and holds the wall together. Our Header Beam Calculator (Tool #5 above) helps you determine the exact size of header you need for your span.

The Bottom Line

Framing is the backbone of the building. Accurate material takeoffs save money, but accurate spacing and load paths save lives. By understanding the relationship between studs, sheathing, and headers, and by adhering to the specific code of your region, you ensure a structure that is straight, strong, and built to last.

Frequently Asked Questions

Framing FAQs

01 What is the standard stud spacing for wall framing?

In the United States, the standard stud spacing is 16 inches on center (o.c.) for load-bearing walls. In the UK and Australia, the standard is typically 400mm or 600mm centers. 24 inches (or 600mm) can also be used in the US if the wall is non-load-bearing or if the sheathing and stud grade are specifically engineered for it.

02 How do I calculate the number of studs I need?

To calculate studs, divide the total length of the wall in inches by the stud spacing (e.g., 16). Add 1 for the end stud. Then, add 2 extra studs for every corner, and 2 extra studs for every window or door opening (to account for the king and jack studs). Always round up to the nearest whole number.

03 What is the difference between a Jack Stud and a King Stud?

A King Stud is a full-height vertical stud that runs from the bottom plate to the top plate and holds the wall structure together. A Jack Stud (or trimmer stud) is a shorter stud nailed to the side of the King Stud that physically supports the horizontal header above a window or door opening.

04 What size header do I need for a 6-foot or 8-foot opening?

The header size depends on the span (opening width) and the load above it. For a typical single-story house with a roof load, a 6-foot opening might require a double 2x8, while an 8-foot opening might require a double 2x10 or 2x12. You must always consult local span tables or a structural engineer to determine the exact size.

05 Should I use OSB or Plywood for sheathing?

Both are acceptable and meet structural codes when installed correctly. OSB (Oriented Strand Board) is generally cheaper and widely used in the US. Plywood is more resistant to moisture and holds nails slightly better, making it preferred in areas with high humidity or for specific structural shear walls. Always ensure the sheathing is rated for the specific application.

06 What is blocking in a stud wall?

Blocking (or noggins in the UK) are short pieces of timber installed horizontally between studs. They are used to: 1) Provide a solid nailing edge for drywall or interior fixtures (like cabinets). 2) Add structural rigidity and prevent studs from twisting. 3) Act as fire blocking to prevent the spread of flames inside the wall cavity.

07 Do I need to use pressure-treated timber for the bottom plate?

Yes, in almost all cases. The bottom plate (or sole plate) sits directly on concrete foundations or subfloors, where it is susceptible to moisture and termites. Building codes universally require pressure-treated (PT) timber for any wood that comes into direct contact with concrete or masonry.
