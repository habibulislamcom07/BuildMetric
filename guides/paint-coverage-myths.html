Why Paint Coverage Numbers on the Tin Are Often Wrong

What manufacturers mean by "coverage", how surface porosity and colour affect it, and how to calculate a realistic order.

You buy a 5-litre tin of paint. The label says it covers "up to 50 square meters." Your wall is exactly 40 square meters. You do the math, feel confident, and start rolling. Halfway through the second coat, you run out.

If this has happened to you, you are not alone. The coverage numbers printed on the side of paint tins are not lies, but they are also not realistic for most construction sites. They are calculated under ideal, laboratory conditions that almost never exist in the real world.

01. The Manufacturer's Math

When a paint manufacturer calculates coverage (usually expressed as square meters per litre, or square feet per gallon), they do it on a perfectly flat, non-porous, uniform test panel.

They apply the paint at a very specific, controlled wet-film thickness. This thickness is the bare minimum required to achieve the stated colour and opacity. In the lab, they do not have to deal with textured drywall, porous masonry, or a painter who is rolling it on slightly thicker to "make sure it covers."

In reality, the number on the tin represents the absolute maximum theoretical coverage. You should always treat it as a "best case scenario," never as the expected outcome.

02. Factor 1: Surface Porosity

The single biggest reason paint runs out early is surface porosity. The paint is absorbed by the surface before it has a chance to form a film on top.

The First Coat is Always Sacrificial

On new drywall, bare plaster, or raw wood, the first coat of paint acts like a sponge. It is absorbed by the material. You will almost always use 30% to 50% more paint on the first coat than on the second.

This is why using a primer is critical. A high-quality primer seals the pores of the drywall or wood. While you pay for the primer upfront, it usually saves you money overall because your expensive topcoat paint sits on the surface instead of soaking into the wall.

Textured surfaces (like stucco or heavy orange-peel drywall) also increase surface area. A wall with heavy texture can have 20% more physical surface area than a smooth wall, requiring more paint to cover the peaks and valleys.

03. Factor 2: Colour Changes

If you are painting a room, the colour you are painting over matters just as much as the colour you are painting with.

  • Light over Dark: Painting white over a deep navy blue will almost certainly require three coats to achieve a solid, opaque finish. The dark pigment bleeds through the first coat.
  • Dark over Light: Painting a dark colour over a light wall is much easier. Two coats are usually sufficient because the dark paint covers the light background very quickly.
  • Vibrant Reds and Yellows: These colours often have poor natural opacity (hiding power). A bright red paint might cover in three or four coats, even over a white primer, because the pigments themselves are somewhat translucent.

In these scenarios, using a tinted primer (a primer that has been tinted to a shade close to your final colour) can drastically reduce the number of topcoats required, saving you both time and paint.

04. Calculating a Realistic Order

So, how do you calculate what you actually need? The rule of thumb is to take the manufacturer's stated coverage and apply a realistic deduction.

Surface / Situation Deduction from Tin Example (Tin says 10m²/L)
New Drywall / Bare Plaster - 20% (if not primed) Assume 8m²/L
Textured Walls - 15% Assume 8.5m²/L
Light over Dark Colour - 25% (extra coat) Assume 7.5m²/L
Primed, Smooth Wall (2nd coat) - 5% Assume 9.5m²/L

Always round up to the nearest full tin. It is far better to have half a litre left over for touch-ups later than to run out halfway through a wall. Also, try to buy all your paint in the same batch (check the batch numbers on the tins), as there can be very slight colour variations between different manufacturing runs.

The Bottom Line

The coverage number on the tin is a laboratory ideal, not a site reality. By understanding how porosity and colour affect absorption, and by applying a realistic deduction, you can ensure you order enough paint to finish the job without a last-minute trip to the store.

Frequently Asked Questions

Paint Coverage FAQs

01 How much area does 1 litre of paint cover?

As a general rule of thumb, 1 litre of standard emulsion paint covers approximately 10 to 12 square metres (about 100 to 130 square feet) per coat. However, this is the manufacturer's ideal laboratory figure. On rough or porous surfaces, expect the coverage to drop to 6 to 8 square metres per litre for the first coat.

02 Should I use a primer to save paint?

Yes, absolutely. Primer is significantly cheaper than topcoat paint. If you apply expensive topcoat directly onto bare drywall or wood, the porous surface absorbs it like a sponge. A primer seals the surface, meaning your topcoat stays on the surface. This usually reduces the number of topcoats required by at least one.

03 Why do I need more paint for a dark colour?

If you are painting a dark colour over a light wall, the dark paint covers the light background very quickly. But if you are painting a light colour (like white or yellow) over a dark wall (like navy blue), the dark pigment will bleed through. It often takes three or four coats to achieve full opacity, requiring significantly more paint.

04 Do textured walls use more paint?

Yes. A textured wall (like orange-peel drywall or stucco) has a much higher surface area than a smooth wall. The paint has to fill in the peaks and valleys. On heavily textured surfaces, you should estimate at least 15% to 20% more paint than you would for a perfectly smooth wall of the same dimensions.

05 Is it better to buy extra paint for touch-ups?

Yes, it is always recommended to buy one extra tin from the same batch. Paint colours can fade slightly over time, and if you need to touch up a scuff mark months later, fresh paint from a new batch might not match perfectly. Keep the leftover paint in a well-sealed container.

06 Does the colour of the undercoat matter?

It can. Using a grey primer under a deep red or dark blue topcoat will require fewer topcoats than using a white primer. Some manufacturers offer tinted primers designed specifically to match the topcoat colour, which can save you both time and money on large projects.

07 How many coats of paint are usually needed?

For a standard interior wall in good condition, applying one coat of primer followed by two coats of topcoat is the industry standard. This provides a durable, washable finish and ensures the true colour of the paint is visible without any background bleed-through.
