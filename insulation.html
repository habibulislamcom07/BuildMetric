The Ultimate Guide to Insulation Estimation

Insulation is the most cost-effective way to reduce energy bills and improve home comfort. Whether you are blowing fiberglass into an attic or installing batts in a wall, the key metric is the R-Value (thermal resistance). Our calculators help you determine the exact number of bags, rolls, or sheets needed. Key Rule: Never compress insulation, as it reduces its R-Value.

Understanding R-Value and U-Value

Insulation performance is measured by its resistance to heat flow. This is expressed as an R-Value in the US and Australia. The higher the R-Value, the better the insulation.

In the UK and parts of Europe, insulation is measured by its U-Value (thermal transmittance). The lower the U-Value, the better the insulation.

To convert between the two: U-Value = 1 / R-Value (using metric units). Our Blown-In Insulation Calculator allows you to input your target R-Value and calculates the exact bags needed.

Global Insulation Standards

Recommended R-Values vary drastically by climate zone.

  • USA: The Department of Energy (DOE) recommends R-30 to R-49 for warm climates (Zone 1-2) and R-49 to R-60 for cold climates (Zone 5-8).
  • UK: Building regulations (Approved Document L) require a U-Value of 0.11 to 0.16 W/m²K for roofs and 0.18 to 0.26 for walls.
  • Australia: The National Construction Code (NCC) sets minimum R-Values based on climate zone, typically R-3.5 to R-6.0 for roofs.

The Math Behind Blown-In Insulation

Blown-in insulation (fiberglass or cellulose) is sold by weight, but applied by volume. The manufacturer provides a coverage chart that tells you how many square feet one bag will cover at a given depth (which corresponds to an R-Value).

For example, a standard 25lb bag of fiberglass might cover 40 sq ft at R-38, or 30 sq ft at R-49. The deeper you want the insulation, the fewer square feet one bag will cover.

Our Blown-In Insulation Calculator uses these standard industry yields to calculate the exact number of bags required. It also factors in existing insulation depth, so you don't over-order.

The Role of Air Sealing

Insulation slows down heat transfer, but it does not stop air movement. Before blowing insulation, you must seal all gaps around plumbing, wiring, and recessed lights. Warm air leaking into the attic bypasses the insulation entirely, reducing its effectiveness.

In cold climates, a vapor barrier is also essential to prevent moisture from condensing inside the wall or attic. For details on drywall and wall prep, use our Drywall Calculator.

Types of Insulation: Which is Right for You?

There is no single "best" insulation. The right choice depends on where you are installing it and your budget.

1. Fiberglass Batts (Blankets)

The most common and affordable insulation. Batts come in pre-cut rolls or panels that fit between studs and joists. They are easy to install in walls and ceilings. However, they must be installed perfectly without gaps or compression, or they lose R-value.

2. Blown-In Insulation

Blown-in insulation (fiberglass or cellulose) is the best choice for attics and retrofitting existing walls. It can be blown into tight spaces and around obstructions, creating a seamless blanket. Cellulose (recycled paper) is denser and better at blocking airflow than fiberglass. Use our Blown-In Insulation Calculator for exact bag counts.

3. Spray Foam

Spray foam is the most expensive option, but it provides the highest R-value per inch and acts as an air barrier. It is applied as a liquid that expands into a solid foam. It is ideal for rim joists, cathedral ceilings, and sealing difficult cavities.

4. Rigid Foam Boards

Rigid foam boards (EPS, XPS, Polyiso) are used for exterior sheathing, basements, and concrete slabs. They provide excellent R-value and are moisture-resistant. They are often used in combination with batt or blown-in insulation.

The Critical Role of Attic Ventilation

Insulation works hand-in-hand with ventilation. If your attic is not properly ventilated, heat and moisture will build up, reducing the insulation's effectiveness and causing roof damage.

The International Residential Code (IRC) requires a minimum of 1 square foot of net free ventilation for every 150 square feet of attic floor area. This is achieved with a combination of intake vents (soffit vents under the eaves) and exhaust vents (ridge vents at the peak or gable vents).

When blowing insulation into the attic, you must install baffles at the eaves. Baffles are foam or plastic channels that keep the insulation from blocking the airflow from the soffit. Our Blown-In Insulation Calculator assumes you are installing baffles to maintain a 1-inch air gap.

Frequently Asked Questions

Insulation Calculator FAQs

Direct answers to common questions about R-values, blown-in bags, and installation.

01

What R-value do I need for my attic?
R-value requirements depend on your climate zone. In the US, the DOE recommends R-30 to R-49 for warm climates and R-49 to R-60 for cold climates. In the UK, a U-value of 0.11 to 0.16 is standard. Use our Blown-In Insulation Calculator for exact bags.
02

How many bags of blown-in insulation do I need?
The number of bags depends on your target R-value and the coverage per bag. A standard 25lb bag covers about 40 sq ft at R-38. For a 1,000 sq ft attic, you would need roughly 25 bags. Use our Blown-In Insulation Calculator for an exact count.
03

What is the difference between R-value and U-value?
R-value measures thermal resistance (higher is better), used in the US and Australia. U-value measures thermal transmittance (lower is better), used in the UK. To convert, U-value = 1 / R-value (in metric units).
04

Can I add blown-in insulation over existing insulation?
Yes, as long as the existing insulation is dry, mold-free, and not compressed. Adding blown-in insulation over old batts is a common way to boost R-value. Use our Blown-In Insulation Calculator to find the additional bags needed.
05

What is the best type of insulation?
There is no single best insulation. Fiberglass batts are cheap and easy for walls. Blown-in fiberglass or cellulose is ideal for attics. Rigid foam boards are best for exterior sheathing and basements. Spray foam is the most airtight but also the most expensive.
06

Do I need a vapor barrier with insulation?
In cold climates, a vapor barrier is installed on the warm side of the insulation to prevent moisture from condensing inside the wall. In warm climates, vapor barriers are often omitted. Always check local building codes.
07

How long does insulation last?
Fiberglass and mineral wool insulation can last 50+ years if not disturbed. Cellulose can settle over time, losing some R-value. Spray foam can last the life of the building. Insulation loses effectiveness if it gets wet or is compressed.
