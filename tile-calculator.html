Tiling a floor or wall is one of the most satisfying DIY projects, but it is also one of the most unforgiving when it comes to estimating materials. Tiles are sold in boxes, and running out halfway through a room is a nightmare. Not only will you have to pause your project, but the new boxes you buy might be from a different dye lot, resulting in a slight color mismatch that is visible forever.

Conversely, over-ordering means you are paying for heavy boxes of tiles that will just sit in your garage. To accurately answer the question "how many tiles do I need?", you need to account for the room's square footage, the size of the tile, the width of the grout lines, and a realistic waste factor. This guide explains exactly how to calculate tile quantities like a professional tiler.

1. How to Calculate Your Room's Square Footage

The first step in any tile estimation is finding the total area you need to cover. For a standard rectangular room, you simply measure the length and the width in feet (or meters), and multiply them together.

For example, a bathroom that is 10 feet long and 8 feet wide has a total area of 80 square feet.

However, real-world rooms are rarely perfect rectangles. If you have an L-shaped room, a closet, or a bay window, you must divide the floor plan into smaller rectangles, calculate the area of each one, and add them together. Do not forget to measure the walls if you are installing wall tiles (shower surrounds, backsplashes). For wall tiles, calculate the height from the floor to the ceiling (or to the tile border) and multiply by the width of the wall.

2. Understanding Tile Sizes (US vs UK vs AU)

The size of the tile you choose drastically affects the number of tiles you need to order. A larger tile covers more area, meaning you need fewer pieces, but larger tiles often result in more waste because offcuts are less usable.

Region Common Sizes Best Used For
United States 12"x12", 24"x24", 3"x6" Residential floors, subway backsplashes.
United Kingdom 300x300mm, 600x600mm Standard metric sizing for floors and walls.
Australia 300x600mm, 600x600mm Large format tiles for modern bathrooms.

Pro Tip: Always check the box. Tiles are usually sold in "coverage per box" (e.g., 1 box covers 15 sq ft). Our calculator allows you to input exactly how many tiles or square feet come in a box so you don't have to guess at the store.

3. The Crucial Role of Grout Lines

Most DIYers forget about grout lines when calculating tile quantities. Grout lines are not just for aesthetics; they allow the tiles to expand and contract with temperature changes without cracking.

If you are using a standard 1/8" (3mm) grout line with 12x12 tiles, the grout line adds a small amount of area. However, if you are using a wider 1/4" (6mm) grout line, or if you are using a very small mosaic tile (like 2x2 inches), the grout lines can account for 10% to 15% of the total surface area. Our calculator automatically factors the grout line into the tile dimensions to give you a precise count.

Grout Estimation: Grout is sold in bags. As a rough estimate, a 10 lb bag of grout will cover about 50 to 100 square feet of tile, depending on the tile size and grout width. Smaller tiles and wider grout lines require more grout.

4. Calculating the Waste Factor

You cannot simply order the exact number of tiles for your square footage. You must account for cuts, breakage, and mistakes. The waste factor is the percentage of extra material you buy to cover this.

  • Standard Layout (10% waste): Used for a straight lay (grid pattern) in a simple rectangular room. You will have some offcuts at the edges of the room.
  • Diagonal Layout (15% waste): Setting tiles at a 45-degree angle requires cutting many tiles at the edges. The triangular offcuts are rarely usable.
  • Herringbone or Complex Patterns (20% waste): Herringbone, chevron, and basketweave patterns require a lot of precision cutting and produce significant waste. Always order extra for these layouts.

5. How to Measure a Bathroom or Kitchen Wall

When calculating for wall tiles, do not just measure the length and height. You must subtract the area of the bathtub, the vanity, and any large windows or doors that will not be tiled.

For example, if you are tiling a shower surround: Measure the length of the three walls, multiply by the height of the shower enclosure. Then, deduct the area of the shower door (if tiling around it) or a niche. Add 15% waste for the tricky cuts around plumbing fixtures.

6. Choosing the Right Tile Material

The material of your tile dictates not only where it can be installed but also how much it costs and how difficult it is to cut. Understanding the difference between ceramic, porcelain, and natural stone is crucial for accurate budgeting.

Ceramic Tile

Ceramic tiles are made from red or white clay and fired at relatively low temperatures. They are softer, easier to cut, and generally less expensive. However, they are less dense and more porous than porcelain, meaning they are best suited for indoor walls and light-traffic floor areas (like bathrooms). They are not recommended for outdoor use or freezing climates.

Porcelain Tile

Porcelain is made from a denser, refined clay and fired at much higher temperatures. This makes it highly water-resistant, durable, and stain-proof. Porcelain is excellent for high-traffic areas, outdoor patios, and wet rooms. Note for estimators: Because porcelain is so dense, it requires a wet saw with a diamond blade to cut. It also requires a high-quality modified thinset mortar to adhere properly.

Natural Stone (Marble, Slate, Travertine, Granite)

Natural stone offers a unique, luxurious look, but it comes with a higher price tag and more maintenance. Stone is naturally porous and must be sealed before and after grouting to prevent staining. It is heavier than ceramic, which may require reinforcing the subfloor. If you are estimating for stone, always add an extra 15% waste factor because natural stone often has variations, pits, and irregular edges.

Glass Tile

Commonly used for kitchen backsplashes and pool areas, glass tiles are beautiful but tricky to install. They require a specific white, modified thinset (to prevent the mortar color from showing through) and a specialized grout. Glass tiles are also prone to chipping, so waste factors should be higher.

7. Regional Installation Standards: US vs UK vs AU

Just like roofing or framing, tiling is governed by strict building codes that dictate how materials are installed to prevent cracking and water damage.

United States (ANSI & TCNA)

In the US, tile installation is guided by the ANSI (American National Standards Institute) and the TCNA (Tile Council of North America) handbook. The TCNA outlines specific methods for different substrates. For example, if you are tiling over a wood subfloor, the TCNA mandates the use of a cement backer board or an uncoupling membrane (like Schluter Ditra) to isolate the tile from the natural expansion and contraction of the wood framing.

United Kingdom (BS 5385)

UK tiling standards are governed by BS 5385 (Wall and floor tiling). The UK heavily emphasizes the distinction between wall and floor adhesives. "Mastic" (ready-mixed adhesive) is common for walls, but BS 5385 strictly requires cement-based powder adhesives for floors and wet areas because mastic will re-emulsify and fail if exposed to water. UK standards also mandate specific background preparation, such as priming plasterboard before tiling.

Australia & New Zealand (AS 3958)

Australian standards are dictated by AS 3958 (Ceramic tiles - Guide to installation). One unique feature of Australian tiling is the strict requirement for "waterproofing" (membrane systems) in all wet areas (bathrooms, laundries, balconies). AS 3958 mandates that a waterproof membrane be painted on the substrate, and the tile adhesive must be compatible with that specific membrane.

8. Cost Estimation: Tiles, Adhesive, and Labor

Budgeting for a tiling project involves more than just the cost of the tiles. You must factor in underlayment, adhesive, grout, and labor.

Material Costs (Per Square Foot/Meter)

  • Ceramic (US): $1.00 - $3.00 per sq ft. (UK: £15 - £30 per sq m).
  • Porcelain (US): $3.00 - $7.00 per sq ft. (UK: £25 - £50 per sq m).
  • Natural Stone (US): $5.00 - $15.00+ per sq ft. (UK: £40+ per sq m).
  • Thinset/Adhesive: A 50lb bag costs $15-$25 and covers about 90 sq ft.
  • Grout: A 10lb bag costs $12-$20.

Labor Costs

Tiling is a highly skilled trade. If you are hiring a professional tiler, expect to pay $4.00 to $12.00 per square foot in the US. In the UK, tilers charge £150 to £300 per day. In Australia, tiling labor ranges from AUD $40 to $80 per square meter. Labor costs increase significantly for complex patterns (herringbone), large-format tiles, and wall tiling (as gravity makes it harder).

9. The Final Checklist Before Ordering

Before you finalize your order with the tile supplier, ensure you have completed these steps:

  1. Check Dye Lots: When you receive your tiles, ensure the shade numbers (dye lots) on all boxes match. Tiles from different batches can have slight color variations.
  2. Order the Extra Box: Even with perfect math, things break. Always keep 1 or 2 unopened boxes in storage for future repairs.
  3. Buy the Backer Board: If tiling a floor, have your cement board or uncoupling membrane ready before you start. Our calculator does not include underlayment.
  4. Check Adhesive Compatibility: Ensure your thinset is rated for your specific tile (e.g., porcelain requires modified thinset; glass requires white thinset).

With your precise tile count, waste factor, and materials list ready, you can confidently begin your project.

Tile Questions Answered

Common questions about calculating tiles, waste factors, and grout.

How many tiles do I need for a 12x12 room?
A 12x12 room has an area of 144 square feet. If you are using standard 12x12 inch tiles (which are 1 sq ft each), you need exactly 144 tiles. However, you must add a 10% waste factor for cuts and mistakes. This brings the total to 158 tiles. If tiles come in boxes of 10, you will need to purchase 16 boxes (160 tiles).
What is the standard waste factor for tiles?
The industry standard waste factor is 10% for a straight lay (grid pattern) in a standard rectangular room. You should increase this to 15% if you are installing tiles diagonally (45 degrees) or in a room with many corners. For complex patterns like herringbone, or when using natural stone, you should budget a 20% waste factor.
How much grout do I need per square foot?
Grout usage depends heavily on tile size and grout line width. For large tiles (e.g., 12x12 or 24x24) with narrow 1/8" grout lines, a 10 lb bag of grout will cover roughly 100 to 150 square feet. For small tiles (like 3x6 subway tiles) or wide grout lines (1/4"), a 10 lb bag may only cover 50 square feet. Always mix a little extra.
Can I use wall tiles on the floor?
Generally, no. Wall tiles are thinner and less durable than floor tiles. They are not designed to withstand foot traffic or the weight of furniture, and they will likely crack or wear out quickly. However, you can use floor tiles on a wall, as they are stronger and more durable (though they are heavier and harder to adhere).
How do I calculate tiles for a shower wall?
Measure the length of each wall around the shower and multiply by the height of the shower enclosure. Add these areas together. Then, deduct the area of any windows, doors, or niches. Because you will be making many cuts around plumbing fixtures (shower valves, spouts), you should increase your waste factor to 15% for shower walls.
Do I need a wet saw to cut porcelain tiles?
Yes, highly recommended. Porcelain is much denser and harder than standard ceramic tile. A manual snap cutter will often crush or chip porcelain rather than giving a clean cut. A wet saw with a diamond-tipped blade is the only reliable way to make straight cuts in porcelain. For curved cuts (like around pipes), you will need an angle grinder with a diamond blade.
Should I buy extra boxes of tiles?
Always. Tiles are manufactured in batches called "dye lots," and there can be slight color variations between batches. If you break a tile in the future or need to make a repair, you may not be able to find the exact color match in a new batch. It is highly recommended to keep 1 to 2 extra boxes of unopened tiles stored in your garage for future touch-ups.
