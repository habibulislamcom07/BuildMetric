A retaining wall is a structural system designed to hold back soil. Whether you are terracing a sloped backyard, building a raised garden bed, or creating a flat driveway on a hillside, a retaining wall transforms unusable land into functional outdoor space. But unlike a decorative garden wall, a retaining wall must be engineered to withstand the immense pressure of wet soil and water.

The most common question we hear is: "How many blocks do I need for my retaining wall?" The answer depends on the wall's dimensions, the block size, and the foundation depth. If you under-order blocks, you'll have a half-finished wall exposed to the elements. If you over-order, you're stuck with heavy, expensive blocks that are difficult to return.

1. The Math: Calculating Wall Area

To calculate blocks, you first need to determine the wall's face area (the visible surface).

Face Area (sq ft) = Length (ft) × Height (ft)

Example: A wall that is 20 feet long and 3 feet high has a face area of 60 square feet.

Once you have the face area, divide it by the coverage of a single block. Standard retaining wall blocks (like those from Allan Block or Keystone) cover about 1 square foot each. So, 60 sq ft = 60 blocks. However, this is only the beginning. You must also account for:

  • Buried Blocks: The first course of blocks must be completely buried below grade. Add 1 extra row.
  • Caps: The wall caps (which sit on top) have a different coverage rate. Always calculate them separately.
  • Waste Factor: Add 5% for straight walls and 10-15% for curved walls, as curved walls require cutting blocks.

2. Foundation Depth and Gravel Base

The foundation of a retaining wall is the most critical component. If the base is not deep enough, the wall will eventually lean or collapse.

Rule of Thumb: The base trench must be dug at least 6 inches deep (plus 1 inch for every 1 foot of wall height). For a 3-foot wall, the trench should be 6 + 3 = 9 inches deep, plus the height of the first buried course. The trench should be filled with 3/4" crushed gravel and compacted thoroughly.

Gravel Math: To calculate the gravel volume, use the trench dimensions:
Trench Volume (cu ft) = Length (ft) × Width (ft) × Depth (ft)
Divide by 27 to convert to cubic yards. Order 10% extra for compaction.

3. The Importance of Drainage

Water is the enemy of a retaining wall. When soil becomes saturated, it becomes incredibly heavy and exerts hydrostatic pressure on the wall. To relieve this pressure, you must install proper drainage.

  • Drainage Gravel: Fill the space directly behind the blocks with 12 inches of clean, free-draining gravel.
  • Drainage Pipe (French Drain): Lay a perforated PVC pipe at the base of the wall, wrapped in landscape fabric to prevent it from clogging with silt. Slope it to drain to a safe area.
  • Drainage Fabric: Place landscape fabric between the drainage gravel and the native soil to prevent the soil from washing into the gravel and clogging it.
  • Weep Holes: If using solid concrete blocks, leave small holes at the base for water to escape. (Modern segmental blocks have integrated weep holes).

4. Geogrid: When Do You Need It?

Geogrid is a mesh-like synthetic material that reinforces the soil behind the wall. It is required for walls that exceed a certain height (usually 3 to 4 feet in the US). Geogrid is laid horizontally between the blocks and extends back into the soil, tying the wall to the earth behind it.

Important: If your wall will be over 4 feet tall, or if there is a heavy load (like a driveway or a vehicle) behind the wall, you must consult a structural engineer. A simple block calculator is not sufficient for engineered walls.

5. Choosing the Right Retaining Wall Block

There are two main categories of retaining wall systems: Segmental Blocks and Concrete Masonry Units (CMU / Cinder Blocks).

Segmental Retaining Wall (SRW) Blocks

These are the most common for residential landscaping. They feature a "lip" or "pin" on the back that locks them into the course below, creating a strong, gravity-based wall. They come in various textures (like chiseled stone or smooth concrete) and are available in straight or curved units.

Pros: Easy to install (dry-stacked, no mortar), flexible (can curve easily), and aesthetically pleasing.
Cons: More expensive per block than CMU.

Concrete Masonry Units (CMU)

These are the standard "cinder blocks" you see on construction sites. They are cheaper but require a concrete footing and mortar to hold them together. They are often used for structural walls or painted walls.

Pros: Cheaper materials, very strong when reinforced with rebar.
Cons: Require concrete footing, need mortaring, harder to curve.

6. Regional Retaining Wall Standards (US/UK/AU)

Retaining walls are structural elements, and their requirements vary significantly across the globe.

United States (IRC & NCMA)

In the US, retaining walls under 4 feet (measured from the bottom of the footing to the top of the wall) generally do not require a building permit or engineering. However, walls over 4 feet, or walls supporting a surcharge (like a driveway or slope above), must be engineered. The NCMA (National Concrete Masonry Association) provides detailed design manuals for SRW blocks.

United Kingdom (BS 8002)

The UK follows BS 8002 (Code of practice for earth retaining structures). UK building regulations require structural calculations for walls over 1.4 meters (4.5 feet). UK standards also place a heavy emphasis on drainage and the use of geotextile membranes to prevent hydrostatic pressure.

Australia (AS 4678)

Australia follows AS 4678 (Earth-retaining structures). Australian councils are very strict about retaining walls on property lines, often requiring a development application (DA) for walls over 600mm (2 feet) if they are on a boundary. Australian standards also require specific drainage details and compaction testing.

7. Cost Estimation

Retaining walls are a significant investment, but they add usable square footage to your property.

Material Costs

  • SRW Blocks (US): $3 to $7 per block. ($10 to $25 per square foot of wall face).
  • CMU Blocks (US): $1.50 to $3.00 per block.
  • UK Blocks: £5 to £12 per block (often sold as "sleepers" or concrete blocks).
  • AU Blocks: AUD $8 to $15 per block (often sold as "Besser Blocks" or "Garden Walls").
  • Drainage Gravel: $30-$50 per cubic yard.
  • Geogrid: $20-$40 per roll (covers 100-200 sq ft).

Labor Costs

Installing a retaining wall is heavy work. If you hire a professional landscaper or mason, expect to pay:

  • US: $20 to $40 per square foot (installed, including excavation and gravel).
  • UK: £150 to £300 per day for a landscaper.
  • Australia: AUD $150 to $300 per square meter (installed).

8. Common Retaining Wall Mistakes

  1. Not Digging Deep Enough: The first course must be buried. If it's not, the wall can slide out at the base.
  2. Skipping the Gravel Base: Placing blocks directly on soil will cause the wall to settle and tilt.
  3. No Drainage: This is the #1 cause of retaining wall failure. Without a gravel backfill and a French drain, the wall will eventually fail.
  4. Not Compacting the Base: The soil under the trench must be compacted with a plate compactor. If you skip this, the wall will sink.
  5. Overloading the Wall: Placing heavy loads (like vehicles) behind a wall that was designed only for soil will cause failure.

Retaining Wall Questions Answered

Common questions about block counts, foundation depth, and drainage.

01 How many blocks do I need for a 20-foot retaining wall?
For a 20-foot wall that is 3 feet high, you need to cover 60 square feet. Using standard retaining wall blocks (which cover 1 sq ft each), you need 60 blocks. However, you must add one extra row for the buried base (about 20 blocks), plus a 10% waste factor. Total: 88 blocks.
02 How deep should the gravel base be for a retaining wall?
The base trench must be dug at least 6 inches deep, plus an extra inch for every foot of wall height. So, for a 3-foot wall, dig the trench 9 inches deep (6 + 3 = 9). Fill it with 3/4" crushed gravel and compact it thoroughly with a plate compactor before laying the first block.
03 What if my blocks are a custom size?
If your blocks are not a standard size (e.g., cinder blocks, timber sleepers, or large-format stone), you must calculate the block's face area manually. Select "Custom Size..." in the calculator's block dropdown and enter your block's exact dimensions. Our calculator will then use that custom size to give you the exact number of blocks required.
04 Do I need a drainage pipe behind the wall?
Yes, absolutely. Water is the #1 cause of retaining wall failure. You must install a perforated drainage pipe (French drain) at the base of the wall, wrapped in landscape fabric and surrounded by clean gravel. This relieves hydrostatic pressure from wet soil and prevents the wall from bulging or collapsing.
05 When do I need a geogrid for a retaining wall?
Geogrid is generally required for walls that exceed 3 to 4 feet in height, or whenever there is a heavy load (like a driveway or a vehicle) behind the wall. Geogrid reinforces the soil and ties the wall to the earth behind it. Always check your local building codes and the block manufacturer's installation guide for specific geogrid requirements.
06 How much gravel do I need for backfill?
You need a minimum of 12 inches of gravel backfill directly behind the retaining wall blocks. The volume depends on the wall length and height. For a 20-foot wall that is 3 feet high, you need about 1.5 to 2 cubic yards of clean, free-draining gravel. Always add a 10% buffer for compaction.
07 Can I build a retaining wall without a permit?
In most jurisdictions (US, UK, AU), walls under 3 to 4 feet (about 1 meter) do not require a building permit. However, any wall that is taller, supports a structure (like a driveway or house), or is located on a property line, will almost certainly require a permit and structural engineering. Always check with your local council before digging.
